Pass 189

Not weather

The causeway went under an hour ago, so I'm here till the ebb. The lamp is low and the water is loud on the stones below.

The register took me a quarter hour. Water against the table, a finger over and falling. Vine quiet the whole run, and I walked it at the grafts before the light went. Kell answered clear, Low Ford answered clear, and I hung nothing out.

Then the last line. It isn't in the ruling and never has been, so I write it out by hand, the way everybody in this room always has.

Sky-sound the whole dark, as before.

As before. It sits at the foot of the page because it isn't anybody's column. It's on the band and not on the vine, and Kell has it and Low Ford has it and we have it, all at once and none of us first. A thing with no first in it tells this station nothing. It has never once come ahead of a wave.

Sael says weather. So did every keeper before me, at the foot of the same page, every night since the roof went on. That's the longest run of writing anybody on this coast has got. Longer than the tide-book. Her guild has a table for it and it has never once come off that table. Nobody looks at it. Why would they.

There's a plainer reason the book holds every one of those nights as as before and not one line of what was in it. Nobody had anything to write it in. The signal-book has a mark for every sound our sets can make and not one of them fits. It doesn't use our pitches. It uses four that are nobody's.

You can write down that a thing was there. You can't write down a sound when you haven't got a mark for it.

So I ruled four, years ago now, on the back of a spoiled page, because the watch is long and writing keeps me awake on the stool better than not writing does. It cost me an evening. Anybody in this room since the roof went on could have done the same on any night she liked. I don't know if that makes me the first of us or just the latest.

Here is tonight, in those four marks. The page starts where my pen came in, which is nowhere in particular, because there is nowhere in particular to come in:

One saying, and then the same saying, and then it again, until the lamp went.

That much has been true since my first year up here. Whatever it is saying it says over and over, and after a season it changes and says the new thing over and over.

For eleven years I could not have written it out the way I just have. What came round was long. Long past holding. It would come back and I'd have lost the front of it already.

Then at the turn of the season it came round short. Short enough to hold whole, first time through, and short enough to measure. Eighteen tones, and then the same eighteen again.

And I still can't tell you where it starts. A thing that goes round and round has no first mark in it. I can cut it at any of those eighteen places, and every cut gives me the same round said a different way about, and not one of them is more the beginning than another. No gap in it anywhere. Nothing set between one and the next.

That is my whole trouble and it is not a small one.

It has changed once more since, and the new one is only a little longer.

So it has stopped doing what it was doing, and it's starting again from almost nothing. I've sat with that for a month.

I've watched weather my whole life and weather turns and turns back. It doesn't spend an age getting complicated and then put the whole lot down and start again from the smallest thing it's got. That isn't a thing weather does. And weather smears. It takes whatever the band will give it, and in eleven years this has never once reached for a fifth mark.

Sael says a tired ear hears its own name in the rain, and maybe it does. But I have eleven years of this in my own figures and I went back along the whole of it tonight. Nothing in there is wasted. Nothing doubles back. Nothing sits idle.

It starts clean from its plainest place. You'd only build a thing that way so somebody could walk into it. That's a road, and somebody lays a road.

So I took a second book out of the press and ruled the first page, and this is that page. I've headed it for the pass, the way the station heads everything, and this is the hundred and eighty-ninth since the post was roofed. The post's paper, the post's ink, hours the coast is paying for. If the steward asks I'll tell her. She won't ask.

I don't know who laid this and I don't know if they're still out there. They might have gone under a long time ago. What I can do is put every step down plain. Then the fellow after me starts where I stopped. She doesn't have to start over.

Three watches on the same stretch and I still can't get it down clean. It's the turn I want, the place where it stops and begins again, and my ear runs ahead of my hand every time.

That would be the end of it if the thing said a stretch once and moved on. It doesn't. Whatever it is on it stays on, and says it again, and again, for a season, and only then changes. So I'm not chasing anything. I take down tonight what I took down last night and the night before that, and I lay the three side by side and keep what two of them agree on.

Ren copies beside me now. She's nine years taken, her hand is fairer than mine, and she doesn't get bored, which I do. Two copies catch what one runs past, and where we differ we mark the spot and take it again.

We split the tides between us, and that isn't for my sake. A post has to be covered, because the water doesn't keep to a table, and two is what the coast pays for. It only means that when one of us is out on the wall the other is in the holding anyway, sat beside the instrument in the dark with nothing else to do.

First statements

The pieces have starts in common: the same short run at the head of a lot of them, unchanged, and then a longer run behind it that's different every time. I can't read any of it, and that doesn't stop me copying it. The sender keeps each run to the same tones wherever it turns up, so I can take one down whole and true long before I know what it says. And it holds a saying a season before it moves, so I get it wrong on the first night and right by the fourth. That is the whole craft so far and it's enough.

And the lone marks sort themselves out. Every cup wears one in front, bar one. The doubled-shut I cut the seam at wears nothing at all, and that is half of why I trust the cut. The two don't fall the same way either. One goes in front of a settled few, a handful of runs that keep coming back without changing; the other goes in front of everything else, and those vary without end.

My guess is that the settled few are its own names, worn like a label and not counted with, and the rest are numbers. I can't turn a single one of them into a count I could say out loud. I'm holding both guesses loosely.

I've no name for any of the sender's own marks and no way to get one. Mine I can rule and call what I like. Its I can only draw as they come and point at, so that is what I do, and where I point at one below I mean the shape and nothing more. Here are the first two pieces in my figures:

The second piece runs longer than the first and looks to hold more than one part. I've met it once, and once reads nothing. So it goes down whole and unread and I wait.

There are two kinds of waiting here and I want them kept apart on this page, because I muddled them myself for a month.

The near one is a season. It is still saying this and it will be saying it tomorrow, so a poor copy is nothing to be afraid of. I sit down again and take it again. That gets me a true copy. It does not get me a reading.

The far one is the other. This thing put down everything it had and started over from its own beginning, with me sat in front of it, so whatever I can't read now it will come round to saying again. Not tomorrow. Ren asked how long and I said I don't know, and I don't, and I've done the arithmetic twice since and I don't think it falls in her watch or in mine.

She said she'd copy it either way.

Sael sat the watch with me. She came up wet and kept her coat on the whole of it, and before I could start she asked whether I'd eaten. I said I had. I hadn't.

Then she counted the steps down with me, twice over, and she didn't call one of them a word. The tide keeps steps, she said. The year keeps steps. A thing can climb in good order and mean no more by the climbing than the moon does.

She has the better of me on the hours and we both know it. It has never come off her table. It is up whenever we are, it doesn't rise and it doesn't set, and it is in every post at once with nothing to choose between them. Nothing on this coast looks more like a cycle than a thing that keeps better time than the water. So I've stopped arguing the hours with her.

What I put to her instead was the list. A cycle doesn't skip. The water doesn't leave out every third turn. This left out everything in between, went up the ladder in order, and stopped where it stopped.

She said a ladder is not a voice. She said it twice, and the second time she was being kind about it.

I won't move her and I don't think I'll move the ones after her either. The tablers will be reading this coast's cycles when my line is dust, and they'll read this the same way and call my road a long tide. So into the book goes the shape, and the steps, and Sael's warning beside them in her own figures, because whoever comes after me should have the strongest thing anybody ever said against this. I think she's wrong. I can't show her why yet.

Pass 228

The unbreakable

Another new mark, another thinned list under it, and this one keeps no step I can see coming:

{{EX:11}}

Two, three, five, seven, eleven, thirteen, and on. Each one breaks into nothing but itself and one. It's shown me a long row of them, longer than showing off would need.

I took it to Sael before I took it anywhere else, because a ladder is not a voice and I have been waiting seven passes to answer that.

She read down my row. Then she went and got their own, which her guild has kept since before this post had a roof, because the cycle-work needs the unbreakables for reckoning when two rounds come true together.

Hers had every number mine had. Of course it did. They are the unbreakables. They belong to anybody who goes looking.

She said, so it can count. So can I.

I hadn't thought of it and I should have, and I sat with it on the stair a long while before I saw what I still had. It isn't that the numbers are ours or theirs. It's that something is putting them in order, one after the next, and going on doing it in the dark whether or not there is anybody at this end of it.

I said that to her. She said that is a great deal to hang on a list.

Two of hers aren't unbreakable at all, past the hundredth. I showed her which two and how. She wrote it in the margin of the guild's book in her own hand and thanked me, and did not give an inch on the other thing, and I would think less of her if she had.

So that's the first thing off this road that was worth anything to anybody. Forty years of nights, and it fixed two numbers in a book. I'm putting that down flat, because nobody after me should expect better than that, and nobody after me should decide it was nothing either.

I'm long taken now and the stair takes me longer than it used to. Whoever sent this has been dead a long while and it's still teaching and it asks nothing back. I won't see the end of the road. I've set every step down plain the way I said I would, and Ren reads my figures better than I write them now.

Tonight I gave her the watch. She asked what a keeper does with a thing she can't read. Set it down whole, I said, and wait for it to come round again. Then I put her hands on the rows where mine had been and came down the stair.
