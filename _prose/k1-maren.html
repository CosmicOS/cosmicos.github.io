Not weather

The causeway went under an hour ago, so I'm here till the ebb. The lamp is low and the water is loud on the stones below.

The register took a quarter hour, most of it waiting on Low Ford to answer. The station book has no line for sky-sound, so I write it at the foot by hand, the way everybody in this room always has. Sky-sound the whole dark, as before.

As before. It's on the band, not the vine, and Kell has it and Low Ford has it and we have it, all at once and none of us first. A thing with no first in it tells this station nothing.

Sael says weather, and so did every keeper before me, at the foot of the same page, every night since the roof went on. Her guild has a table for it and it has never once come off that table. Nobody looks at it. Why would they.

There's a plainer reason the book holds every one of those nights as as before and not one line of what was in it. Nobody had anything to write it in. The signal-book has a mark for every sound our sets can make and not one of them fits. It doesn't use our pitches. It uses four that are nobody's.

So I made four marks of my own, one for each of the four sounds, on the back of a spoiled page, years ago now. I did it because the watch is long and writing keeps me awake on the stool. It cost me an evening.

Here is a page of it, in those four marks. It's one saying, said over and over till the lamp went, and I've set down as much of it as the page will hold. It runs long and it is the same thing throughout. The page starts where my pen came in, because there is nowhere in particular to come in:

That much has been true since my first year up here. After a season it changes and says the new thing over and over.

For eleven years what came round was tangled past holding. It would come back and I'd have lost the front of it already. I don't suppose I'm the first in this room to sit with a pen and have a go. Whoever did got a page out of the middle of it, with no way of saying where the middle was. Paper like that gets used for something else. Then at the turn of the season it came round short. Short enough to hold whole, first time through, and short enough to measure. Eighteen tones, and then the same eighteen again.

So it put the whole tangle down and started again from almost nothing. It has been growing ever since. I've sat with that for a month.

I've watched weather my whole life and weather turns and turns back. It doesn't spend an age getting complicated and then put the whole lot down and start again from the smallest thing it's got. And weather smears — it takes whatever the band will give it, and in eleven years this has never once reached for a fifth mark.

Sael says a tired ear hears its own name in the rain, and maybe it does. But I have eleven years of this in my own figures and I went back along the whole of it tonight. It starts clean from its plainest place and builds. Nothing in it is wasted and nothing doubles back.

I'm not going to write down tonight what I think that means. Whatever I put on this page gets read in ninety years as a finding, by somebody who wasn't here and can't ask me. It isn't weather. A thing that puts everything down and starts again from the smallest thing it holds has a beginning, and a beginning is a place you can go in at.

If anybody laid this, I don't know who, or whether they're still out there, or whether they're anything like us. They might have gone under a long time ago. What I can do is put every step down plain.

So I took a second book out of the press and ruled the first page, and this is that page. I've headed it for the pass, the way the station heads everything, and this is the hundred and eighty-ninth since the post was roofed. Under the pass I've put two or three words for what the night was, so a fellow can find the night again without reading her way to it. I'm sixty-one years taken. The post has stood three of me. The post's paper, the post's ink, hours the coast is paying for. If the steward asks I'll tell her. She won't ask.

Three watches on the same stretch and I still can't get it down clean. It's the turn I want, the place where it stops and begins again, and my ear runs ahead of my hand every time.

That would be the end of it if the thing said a stretch once and moved on. It doesn't. Whatever it is on stays on for a season. So I'm not chasing anything. I take down tonight what I took down last night and the night before that, and I lay the three side by side and go along them mark by mark, keeping whatever two of the three have. A slip lands in a different place each night, so it stands out as one page in three and goes in the fire. What I can't catch is two of them slipping in the same place. Then I keep the wrong mark and never know I did.

Ren copies beside me now. She's nine years taken, her hand is fairer than mine, and she doesn't get bored, which I do. Two copies catch what one runs past, and where we differ we mark the spot and take it again.

We split the tides, so whichever of us is off the wall is in the holding anyway, in the dark, with nothing else to do.

Ren asked what it does in the daylight and went and sat a bright day to find out. The same stretch, tone for tone. Her day page has gone in with the other three.

Sael sat the watch with me. She came up wet and kept her coat on the whole of it, and before I could start she asked whether I'd eaten. I said I had. I hadn't.

Then she counted the steps down with me, twice over, and she didn't call one of them a word. The tide keeps steps, she said. The year keeps steps. A thing can climb in good order and mean no more by the climbing than the moon does.

Nothing on this coast looks more like a cycle, and I have stopped arguing the hours with her.

What I put to her instead was the list. A cycle doesn't skip. The water doesn't leave out every third turn. This left out everything in between, went up the ladder in order, and stopped where it stopped.

She said a ladder is not a voice. She said it twice, and the second time she was being kind about it.

I won't move her. So into the book goes the shape, and the steps, and Sael's warning beside them in her own figures, because whoever comes after me should have the strongest thing anybody ever said against this. I think she's wrong. I can't show her why yet.

The unbreakable

Another new mark, another thinned list under it, and this one keeps no step I can see coming:

{{EX:12}}

Two, three, five, seven, eleven, thirteen, and on. Each one breaks into nothing but itself and one. It's shown me a long row of them, longer than showing off would need.

I took it to Sael before I took it anywhere else, because a ladder is not a voice and I have been waiting seven passes to answer that.

She read down my row. Then she went and got their own, which her guild has kept since before this post had a roof, because the cycle-work needs the unbreakables for reckoning when two rounds come true together.

Hers had every number mine had. Of course it did. They are the unbreakables. They belong to anybody who goes looking.

She said, so it can count. So can I.

I hadn't thought of it and I should have. It isn't that the numbers are ours or theirs. It's that something is putting them in order, one after the next, in the dark, whether or not there is anybody at this end of it.

I said that to her. She said that is a great deal to hang on a list.

It is. I'm nearly done now, so it goes down in plain words and whoever holds this book can weigh it against Sael's on the same page.

It starts at nothing. It goes up in order. It skips nothing and it wastes nothing. You only lay a thing out like that so somebody can walk into it.

That's a road.

Two of hers aren't unbreakable at all, past the hundredth. I showed her which two and how. She wrote it in the margin of the guild's book in her own hand and thanked me, and did not give an inch on the other thing, and I would think less of her if she had.

So that's the first thing off this road worth anything to anybody. Forty years of nights, and it fixed two numbers in a book. Nobody after me should expect better than that, or decide it was nothing either.

I'm long taken now and the stair takes me longer than it used to. Whoever sent this has been dead a long while and it's still teaching and asks nothing back. Ren reads my figures better than I write them.

Tonight I gave her the watch. She asked what a keeper does with a thing she can't read. Set it down whole, I said, and wait for it to come round again. Then I put her hands on the rows where mine had been and came down the stair.
