Same, and more, and fewer

I cut her name into the doorstone, then the record above it. The watch changing over, plain, where it can't be mistaken or lost. Nobody did that before me because nobody had to: there was one of us keeping this book, and now there have been two. Things go missing between two. The post took me up at nine passes taken, to copy beside her, and I never left. The cutting came after, and the tide-book presses have been mine since.

I trust a thing cut over a thing said. So this book is going to say less than it did. What I'm sure of, I cut. What I'm not, I leave blank. A blank waits. A wrong mark stays. And the press is stricter than the book. The book gets read from the front, so a page can lean on the page before it. A stone gets found. It has to stand on its own with nothing beside it, and whoever finds it never knew us. So a word never goes on the press without the mark it stands for cut next to it, and nothing goes on at all that needs the book to make sense of it.

One more thing. It goes against the founder.

At 207 she wrote that every cup wears a mark in front. Not the two of the doubled-shut she cut the seam at. Those wear none. She said that was half of why she trusted the cut.

It held for every saying she ever heard. I've been through all thirty-seven. There's no exception in them. Tonight a cup came with nothing in front of it. It isn't the seam. It sits in the middle of a saying like any other.

So that half of her reason is gone. It wasn't wrong when she wrote it. It was true for the whole of her watch. It failed in the second saying of mine.

The cut stands. Here's what holds it up now. Cut at the doubling and every piece closes. Every cup that opens is shut inside the same piece. Nothing left hanging, nothing shut that was never opened. Cut a tone early or a tone late and they come apart. Cups standing open. Lids with nothing under them. I did it wrong on purpose, twice, to see it fail.

Hers was a thing she looked at. Mine I can do again tonight, on tonight's page, and again tomorrow.

Hers stays in the book as she wrote it. Mine goes under it. Neither goes on the press. A reason needs the book beside it. That is my own rule and it binds me too.

Read down the column on the right. On, off, on, off, the whole way. Read down the next and it holds two and turns two. The next, four and four. Every column is the one beside it taken slower, and that is the whole of how the thing writes a number.

There is a second thing in it and it cost me another night. A mark of its own is a cup with a count in it, the same as any other cup. So every one of them has a number, and a number is a thing I can now write in our own figures. Maren named the two she wrote most off the wind on the flats, because a name was all she had to give them. Everything else she could only draw out mark by mark, and there are hundreds.

{{EX:unary-readback-4}}

Seven. That is all it ever was, and it took eighty passes to be able to say so. From tonight any mark it sends goes down in one figure, read or unread, and the way of it is cut into the press so nobody has to take it from me.

What this is worth, plainly. A count of two hundred used to cost two hundred cups and now it costs eight marks. I cut the same figure in a third of the stone and read a page of them at a glance instead of counting my way down. So I took it to the counting-house at Kell on the spring water, with a fair sheet.

They were kind about it. They have a way of writing numbers. It's served them a long time. Their clerks are fast at it and their books all match.

A house doesn't tear up its books because a woman off a rock has a better way. The head clerk asked where I got it. I told her the truth. She said, well. Then she gave me tea. I'm not sour. If a stranger came to me with a better way to cut a press, I'd want to watch her cut one first.

Aud goes down to Kell in the spring. The table-house has taken her on to cut presses. I taught her that, at this bench, and it pays better there than it does here. She copied for me the whole of my watch and never once said the work was worth doing. She took her own sheets with her, which are hers, and I did not ask what was on them.

I'll put down one thing, for whoever is holding this book in a hundred passes. It was better. I offered it. They didn't want it.
