Pass 232

Same, and more, and fewer

I cut her name into the doorstone. Then I cut the record above it. The watch changing hands, plain, where it can't be mistaken or lost. Nobody did that before me. Nobody had to. There was one of us keeping this book. Now there have been two. Things go missing between two. I'm a cutter. The post took me up out of the cutters at forty years taken, to cut presses for the tide-books, and I stayed on.

I trust a thing cut over a thing said. So this book is going to say less than it did. What I'm sure of, I cut. What I'm not, I leave blank. A blank waits. A wrong mark stays.

I copy everything. I read what I can. Those are two jobs. I'm going to stop pretending they're one. A fellow after me might want the run I had no use for tonight. She can't go and hear it again. It comes round, but the round is longer than a life.

Sixty-one is the worst line I've cut. I read it back three times to be sure of the count. That's a poor use of an evening and I did it anyway.

I did one thing with it. I took the run down to the table-house and laid it against the guild's reckoning-board, line for line, as far as their board goes.

Not one disagreement.

I'd half hoped for a disagreement. It would have been something to carry down the stair. Instead the sky and the guild agree about heaping, and the clerk was polite, and I came home on the ebb.

Pass 267

Short figures

It's stopped spelling counts out. All this while, a count came as one small cup for each. A count of nine cost nine cups. A count of forty cost forty. Tonight it set a fistful of marks in place of a whole run and swore the two were the same:

{{EX:9}}

It did it for one. Then two. Then three. Then up past the height where a run gets unwieldy. Every time it laid the short figure and the long run side by side and called them equal. It's teaching me its own shorthand by writing out both and pointing. Then it went and used the short figures in the old workings. Joining with them, heaping with them, like they'd always been there:

{{EX:10}}

What this is worth, plainly. A count of two hundred used to cost two hundred cups. Now it costs eight marks. I cut the same figure in a third of the stone. I can read a page of them at a glance instead of counting my way down it. So I took it to the counting-house at Kell on the spring water, with a fair sheet, and showed them.

They were kind about it. They have a way of writing numbers. It's served them a long time. Their clerks are fast at it and their books all match.

A house doesn't tear up its books because a woman off a rock has a better way. The head clerk asked where I got it. I told her the truth. She said, well. Then she gave me tea. I'm not sour. If a stranger came to me with a better way to cut a press, I'd want to watch her cut one first.

I'll put down one thing, for whoever is holding this book in a hundred passes. It was better. I offered it. They didn't want it.
