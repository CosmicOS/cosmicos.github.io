Pass 544

The same stuff

Cael's put her pen down. She read the whole road's purpose out of one pass and it took the legs from under her. I'm a plainer sort. I came off the quarry and knew what a load weighed years before I knew what it was called. Tonight the message turned to the stuff things are made of, and did something it has never done.

It stopped proving.

It has always shown, and the showing was the proof. Tonight it set marks down and put a sign in front of each. The sign says, near as I can read it: this one I can't show you, take my word. It has never asked for my word before. Not once in the whole of this book.

What it wants me to take is three things, all built the same. A little frame of two slots. In one, a number too small for any name I have. In the other, a plain count. Only three of those ever turn up: one, one turned the other way, and none:

…one of the three, as it comes off the tones; the small number in it runs longer than I will copy…

I cut the take-it-on-trust sign given. The small numbers I can't weigh. Their measure against each other is only figures, and figures I can do. The first thing's number is the second's laid down one thousand eight hundred and thirty-six times. And a tail after that:

Then a maker that builds bigger frames out of two counts, and a row of them, each told from the next by how many of the first thing it holds. One, two, six, seven, eight. A ladder, rung on rung. Then it gathers the rungs into bundles, and for each bundle writes how many of this rung and how many of that.

I can copy every figure true and I have. The three things at the root I can't reach and I don't think anybody at our end can. But the rest wouldn't let me alone. A ladder of things, each told from the next by a plain count, gathered into bundles by small fixed counts. Two of this to one of that. Never a part of one.

I've watched assayers at their balances since my first year in the quarry house, and this had their smell on it. I split stone. I don't weigh airs. So I copied the ladder and the bundles fair and sent the sheet over on the next ebb to old Oram, who keeps the balances at the far settlement. I sent no guess with it. A guess would only have told Oram what I wanted to hear.

Three tides on, their word came back, and it was wrong, and Oram said so themselves eight days later.

The first sheet said the numbers were a scale of hardness. Oram had laid them against the assayer's own scratch-order and got four of the five to sit, and I believed it and got two nights into writing it up. Then the second sheet. The fifth rung wouldn't sit, so they took the scratch-order away and tried the combining weights instead. All five sit. They sit exactly.

I've kept the first sheet in the press with the second. The next fellow should see how well a wrong one can look.

Those numbers beside each rung are weights. And they are weights they hold.

Near one, the inflammable air. Near twelve, the char that blacks whatever it touches. Near fourteen, the greater part of the air we breathe. Near sixteen, the part that feeds a flame.

The message laid out Oram's own shelf, rung by rung, and the bundles are the joinings: the fixed small counts one air or earth takes up another in. That rule is what Oram's whole trade stands on and they have never had a proof of it. One bundle Oram named without stopping. Two of the lightest and one of the flame-feeder.

That is water.

Our water. The sea that takes the causeway twice a day, the rain standing in the quarry pools. It spelled others alongside it, so it was never speaking only to us. It spoke our water plain.

Not all of it came clear to Oram either. The three root things they handed straight back unread. And one rung, a light one near four, they couldn't place at all. Weighed true, sat firm in its row, answering to nothing on their shelf.

Pass 549

Nothing laid against it

Short pass.

It ushered in a new mark, the old gesture that says here comes a sign you haven't met. Then it gave the sign nothing. No doing. No count. No rule to stand for:

And it didn't set it working where I could watch it and take its measure that way, which is the other road it has always given me. It went on and hasn't come back. I've turned it over a whole pass and know no more than when I started. So I've copied it true and left it lying. A fellow with more to go on may make something of it.

Pass 552 · the chinking

Frost in the north chinking, so no reading at all this pass. Spent it packing the gap with tow and clay. Damp comes in through a gap like that and takes a page you can't copy again. Ona lost pages to it. That's the entry.

Pass 554

The switch, and a field to plant it in

Whatever last pass's mark was for, this is what came after, and I wasn't ready. Tonight it built with a thing. A little switch. Two go in, each a plain true or false. One comes out. It showed me every way the two can go and what comes out each way:

One way of the four gives true and only one. When the first holds and the second doesn't. It says yes, unless.

I cut it switch.

A maker knows in the hand what the head is slower at. A switch of exactly this make is enough: lay down enough of them, wire one into the next, and you can build any judgment there is. Its make is no secret either, being cut from marks my line has carried for ages. A doing of two slots that gives the first and the turn-over of the second:

Then it made a place to put them, and I put the pen down. Not a name. Not a slot. A whole flat field, going away in two directions at once, every spot found by where it sits. Lay a value at a spot and come back later and it's still there. An empty field, a figure laid in, drawn out unchanged:

Then it planted switches on the field and swept it. It worked out what every one of them does and gave me the field after:

A part, a ground to lay it on, and a sweep that works the whole ground at once. I went down to the bench after and stood there and didn't make anything, which is not like me. You could cut this one out of tin.

Pass 559

Under the hand

For every pass of this road the message has come single file, a mark then the next, each unfolded in its turn. Tonight it stopped. It laid one thing out flat, with breadth to it as well as length, and left it standing whole. To be taken in and not read.

A height, a width, a stack of numbers, one to a row. I unfolded each row-number into its bare marks and laid them at the width it named, marks standing proud and empty ones lying flat, the rows one above the next. Then I put my hands on them and the numbers stopped being numbers.

What the hands got goes under each figure, before I write another word about it. In the order they got it and nothing added. My hands are quicker than my head at this and the head spoils it.

Three rows, twenty across. Upright stroke, left of middle. One raised mark beside it, to the right. Nothing else on the field.

Nothing in itself. It went through me cold anyway. It can give a shape — laid out with breadth, to be taken whole in a moment, the way your hand knows a mark cut in stone before you've read a word of it. Not spoken across the dark. Shaped across it, and left standing for a hand to find. Then it laid down a big one. A hundred and nine rows tall, a hundred and sixty-nine across. I raised the whole of it and set all three hands down at once. It stood up in me in a few breaths:

Border all round, raised, closed on itself. In from the left edge, one smooth ridge. Middle, where the ridges gather: a knot. Out the right edge, one ridge — smooth, gap, smooth.

Now I'll admit the thing a keeper shouldn't. I felt every part of it plainly and I could not say what I'd felt.

Then, going along it once more, something I knew came up out of it. It's the switch-works. The same kind it planted on the field a few passes back. Only now it isn't planted to be run. It's laid out at size to be found by the hand.

And here's the answer to the doubt I just wrote down. Having laid the shape down, it took it back — ran across the figure a block at a time, drew the switch-works whole out of it, set that against the works it built on the field and swore them equal. It gave me a reading I could feel clearly and still get wrong, and then made it so I couldn't. It can't know what we are, or what we read the world with, so it didn't wager on hands. It laid the shape out for whatever we've got, then tied it to a works, so it comes out the same either way.

You draw a part at full size when you want it made. Somebody on some far side is meant to pick these up.

A pass in this book reads the same whether it cost a night or a fortnight. Mine cost fortnights. So the days go under the figures from here on, with the rest of what my hands did.

Six days' writing. Two on the figures, four on the words.
Pass 564

The both-knot

Tonight it built one part out of the others. Not a fresh figure drawn from nothing. A larger one made of the small switches I already know, wired mouth to mouth into one works. And when I fed it the whole crowd answered as one, with one plain rule:

Two ridges in from the left, not one. Mirrored about the waist. To the middle, a dense knot of switches. Out the right, a single ridge.

Two in, one out. I've felt that shape before, small: it's what the little switch does. Only this is a whole knotted field of them, and laid out it has the very form of the single part it's built from.

It proved the drawing against a works the way it did before. That swearing is one line, the drawing set against the works, the two called the same:

The mark it swore by I cut both-knot. Then the new thing, and it's the pass's heart. It didn't feed it. The little switch it had showed at work so I could read the rule off the answers. This one it drew, and proved, and went quiet, as though what the thing does is my affair now.

So it is. Whenever it proves a drawing it hands me the works entire, so I built the figure a mouth for each input and a place to read what came back, and fed it every way two signals can go:

both-knot
both-knot

Whole only when both come in whole. Any broken one and the answer breaks. That's the whole of what the does. And here's what stopped me. A field of switches I could barely carve, and it answers in four clean lines — the same four the little switch answered in when it was one part and not a hundred. The many parts speak with one voice, and it's a voice I know. Both-or-nothing. The verdict Neru forked out of yes and no a whole age before any of us thought to build it.

So the parts aren't a collection. They're a ladder. It draws a small one, proves it, works it, then builds it into a larger one that hides all that smallness inside a single new rule. A catalogue lies flat. This is being stacked, and a thing gets stacked when it's too big to draw in one piece. I can't see what stands at the top.

Pass 566 · Oram's rung

A sheet back from Oram across the water, unasked. Twenty-two passes on that light rung near four, weighed against everything on their shelf, and it answers to none of them. So they've started a second shelf. For the ones the message names that they cannot find. Three on it now. Three things that are somewhere, and nobody at our end has ever held one.

I sent back that I keep a page like that too and mine is longer. Oram is eighty-one years taken and still sending sheets across the water on the ebb.

Pass 569

Left to my own hands

Two more, both left for me the way the both-knot was. Build it, feed it every way, read the rule off what comes back. Four days.

The both-knot's cousin, so near I had to feel it twice to tell them apart. Only the weave at the middle runs otherwise. Whole when either comes in whole.

The second is the biggest yet, and I half read it before I fed it: the answer-ridge runs out broken and dashed the whole way, same as the very first figure's did. I fed it to be sure. Whole only when neither comes in whole. So I laid out the ones I have. The four ways two signals can come, and under each, what each part makes of it:

both
either
neither

Turn-over. Both. Either. Neither.

I haven't been shown that those are all the ways two things can meet. I'm not writing that they are. But lay them side by side and the space of it feels small, and nearly full. I ruled a fifth row under them and left it blank, in case.

Pass 574

A part with no answer

Tonight it drew a part, proved it, and left it for me. I fed it, and for the first time in all my feeding it wouldn't answer. Not that it gave nothing. That it wouldn't stop. Every part before this settled onto its one value and held there, and that settling is the whole of what an answer is.

This one never quiets. Whole. Swept again, broken. Again, whole. On and on. My log keeps a place at the right of every part where I put what it gives. For this one I couldn't fill it:

ring …

I went to the figure to find out why, sure I'd wired it wrong:

Answer-ridge, followed from the knot. Right, then up, then back along the top. Into the knot again. No edge on it anywhere.

My hands found the reason before my head had it. Every part until now I could trace from its inputs out to an end at the far edge, where the answer waits. This one has no such end. I set a finger on the answer-ridge and followed it and it didn't run to an edge.

It ran back. Curled round through the knot and rejoined itself. A ring. Closed. No ends at all.

There's nowhere to read an answer because the answer runs back into the question. It's wired to feed itself. I've made things that move — the field moved — but I swept the field, and when I stopped, it stopped. This is the first part I've met that moves itself. Start it once and it wants nothing more from me. It would keep turning in an empty room with the door shut and nobody to read it.

I built one, of course. My hands credit nothing until they've made it. Small, out of the bench's own scrap.

Wire, soft iron, two spans, bent cold. No fire on this bench since Cael's time.The spring off a latch that was no use to anybody.Tin, the offcut from the chinking, for the plate.Four pegs, oak, cut fresh. The old ones split.Waxed gut, to hold it while the iron took the bend.

Three days. Whole, broken, whole, broken. No hand near it. It ticks while it does it. I didn't expect that and I can't stop it. Such things don't keep to a station. There were folk at the outer gate this morning, not of the line, come to see the thing that runs by itself. The steward sent them off. Behind them was one she couldn't send off so easily. She didn't ask to see it. She asked to be taught its making. She was told no and she didn't look told no.

I've taken to laying a cloth over it at night. I couldn't say against what. It is going under there while I write this.

Pass 576 · the visitor

A clerk came over on the ebb from the counting-city, with a letter from the steward and a good coat. She wanted the ring. I said it does nothing. It won't settle. It has no answer. I've written as much in the log. She said she didn't want an answer. She wanted a thing that keeps time.

I didn't show her the ring. I showed her the figure of it. I thought a picture would put her off where a working part wouldn't.

She had it in under an hour. She has never heard a tone in her life.

She sat with all three flat on the raised rows. Traced the answer-ridge round to where it curls back into itself. Said, so it never comes to an end, and that's why.

She was right. I'd told her nothing.

She asked to have the figure copied. I said no. She said she'd ask the steward. Then she thanked me and went down to catch the water.

It took me three days to copy the ring's figure true and I'm slow with a pen. A fair hand would do it in an afternoon.

Pass 579

The part that keeps

The steward has written. I may not refuse the counting-city a copy: the post is theirs and what's drawn in the log is the post's. Three by spring. I haven't begun them.

I half expected the next part to be more of the ring's unrest. It was the opposite and I haven't slept:

Another figure with the loops in it, and where the ring was one loop this is two. Crossed and linked, each running back into the other. They hold one another the way two who lean together stand. And when I fed it, it didn't run. A signal to the one side and its answer went whole. Then I took every signal off it. And it stayed whole. I came back a long while later, lamp burned down, and it was holding still. Then a signal to the other side and it went broken, and it kept the broken as faithfully as it had kept the whole:

I put it whole
I take my hands away
I put it broken
I take my hands away

Look at the two rows where my hands are off it. Every part before the ring answered while you asked and forgot the moment you stopped. The ring wouldn't stop. This one stops, and it remembers.

Same switches as all the rest, no new stuff in it, and out of that it has built a past.

The one who wanted the ring's making came back today. Past the gate this time, to the under-steward, with money in it. And talk about what such an engine could do for a house that rules by records. It need never trust a memory again.

She was sent off. But I marked that it was half the line that sent her and not all of it. Some among us have started saying, low, that a thing that never forgets shouldn't be let out past our door. Others that it isn't ours to keep from the world. I take no side. I only build.

Five passes back it made a beat. Tonight, a memory. I've built long enough to know what you have once you set those two side by side. Three copies by spring. I've ruled the first sheet.

Nine days' writing, and the fair copies not begun.
