Same, and more, and fewer

I cut her name into the doorstone, then the record above it. The watch changing over, plain, where it can't be mistaken or lost. Nobody did that before me because nobody had to: there was one of us keeping this book, and now there have been two. Things go missing between two. The post took me up out of the cutters at forty years taken, to cut presses for the tide-books, and I stayed on.

I trust a thing cut over a thing said. So this book is going to say less than it did. What I'm sure of, I cut. What I'm not, I leave blank. A blank waits. A wrong mark stays. And the press is stricter than the book. The book gets read from the front, so a page can lean on the page before it. A stone gets found. It has to stand on its own with nothing beside it, and whoever finds it never knew us. So a word never goes on the press without the mark it stands for cut next to it, and nothing goes on at all that needs the book to make sense of it.

No

A new mark. It goes in front of a saying and turns it over. It sets down a thing that's true, plain, with nothing in front of it. Then it sets down a thing that's false with this mark in front. Now the whole line is true again:

…and so, again and again: the true thing said plain, the two false ones denied…

I cut it deny. It never once stands in front of something true. That's how I know it. That's all I know of it. Then it did the same thing forty more times.

I read six. I copied the rest without reading them.

I copy everything. I read what I can. Those are two jobs and I'm going to stop pretending they're one. A fellow after me might want the run I had no use for tonight, and she can't go and hear it again. It comes round, but the round is longer than a life.

One thing about this book that Maren never wrote down, because she knew it and I had to work it out. It holds the nights something came. Between one entry and the next there are years, and the register was kept every one of them, and the sky-sound was on the band every one of them, and none of it was new. A gap here is not a gap in the watch. It is the watch, doing what it does.

Writing that out long every pass would cost me a day at the stone, so it goes short. And the key goes into the press, cut, because a short line nobody can read is worse than no line at all.

Heaps joined

A new mark, with two counts shut in the cup beside it and a third count standing outside. Change the two and the third changes with them. It shows it and shows it and shows it:

…and more of the same, each true when I count it…

I counted every one off on my feet, twice round for the big ones. They all came true. I cut it join. It doesn't care which of the two I set down first, and it showed me that as well, both ways round, a dozen times, like somebody might have doubted it.

Then I sat a while, because there is another reading and no amount of counting puts it down. Take the big count, lift the small one off, and what is left is the third. Every line up there is true that way too. Set the two inside the other way round and it is still true. Nothing I can count tells the two readings apart.

What I have is the shape, and it is thin. A mark seems to work on what is shut in with it and nothing further out. stands outside both counts and reaches them both. is shut in the cup with its two. The big count is outside, so it should have no reach to it at all. I have never seen a cup like this one stand where a plain count stands. Until I do, I am leaning on a habit and not a rule. Heaps of heaps

Now it heaps a heap. So many of that count, and here's what you get:

…every count against every count, more than I will copy…

It has gone through every count against every count, patiently, since before I took the watch, and it hasn't finished. I copied what I could and entered the rest.

I did one thing with it. I took the run down to the table-house and laid it against the guild's reckoning-board, line for line, as far as their board goes.

Not one disagreement.

I'd half hoped for a disagreement. It would have been something to carry down the stair. Instead the sky and the guild agree about heaping, and the clerk was polite, and I came home on the ebb.

Short figures

It's stopped spelling counts out. All this while, a count came as one small cup for each. A count of nine cost nine cups. A count of forty cost forty. Tonight it set a scatter of marks in place of a whole run and swore the two were the same:

…up to that same height, one short of the feet, and past it — a scatter of marks shrunk each time to a few tones…

It did it for one. Then two. Then three. Then up past the height where a run gets unwieldy. Every time it laid the short figure and the long run side by side and called them equal. It's teaching me its own shorthand by writing out both and pointing. Then it went and used the short figures in the old workings. Joining with them, heaping with them, like they'd always been there:

There is a second thing in it and it cost me another night. A mark of its own is a cup with a count in it, the same as any other cup. So every one of them has a number, and a number is a thing I can now write in our own figures. Maren named the two she wrote most off the wind on the flats, because a name was all she had to give them. Everything else she could only draw out mark by mark, and there are hundreds.

Seven. That is all it ever was, and it took eighty passes to be able to say so. From tonight any mark it sends goes down in one figure, read or unread, and the way of it is cut into the press so nobody has to take it from me.

What this is worth, plainly. A count of two hundred used to cost two hundred cups and now it costs eight marks. I cut the same figure in a third of the stone and read a page of them at a glance instead of counting my way down. So I took it to the counting-house at Kell on the spring water, with a fair sheet.

They were kind about it. They have a way of writing numbers. It's served them a long time. Their clerks are fast at it and their books all match.

A house doesn't tear up its books because a woman off a rock has a better way. The head clerk asked where I got it. I told her the truth. She said, well. Then she gave me tea. I'm not sour. If a stranger came to me with a better way to cut a press, I'd want to watch her cut one first.

Aud goes down to Kell in the spring. The table-house has taken her on to cut presses, which is my old trade and a better living than this one. She copied for me the whole of my watch and never once said the work was worth doing. She took her own sheets with her, which are hers, and I did not ask what was on them.

I'll put down one thing, for whoever is holding this book in a hundred passes. It was better. I offered it. They didn't want it.
