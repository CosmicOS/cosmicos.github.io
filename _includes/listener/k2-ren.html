Same, and more, and fewer

I cut her name into the doorstone, then the record above it. The watch changing over, plain, where it can't be mistaken or lost. Nobody did that before me because nobody had to: there was one of us keeping this book, and now there have been two. Things go missing between two. The post took me up at nine passes taken, to copy beside her, and I never left. The cutting came after, and the tide-book presses have been mine since.

I trust a thing cut over a thing said. So this book is going to say less than it did. What I'm sure of, I cut. What I'm not, I leave blank. A blank waits. A wrong mark stays. And the press is stricter than the book. The book gets read from the front, so a page can lean on the page before it. A stone gets found. It has to stand on its own with nothing beside it, and whoever finds it never knew us. So a word never goes on the press without the mark it stands for cut next to it, and nothing goes on at all that needs the book to make sense of it.

Seven. That is all it ever was, and it took eighty passes to be able to say so. From tonight any mark it sends goes down in reckoning marks, read or unread, and the way of it is cut into the press so nobody has to take it from me.

What this is worth, and it isn't the shortness. Kell writes two hundred — , two marks and done — as quick as I do and has done longer. What it buys is that the working stands on the page. Set two counts one under the other, place under place, and the adding is a thing you do with a pen — and it is still there afterwards, so a fellow who wasn't in the room can go down it and find where you went wrong. I did a season of tide-sums that way to be sure of it. Then I took the sheet down to the counting-house at Kell on the spring water.

They were kind about it. Their clerks work a board and they are fast at it, faster than my pen, and their books all match. So what I brought them wasn't speed. It was the sum lying on the paper afterwards — and nobody in that house has ever wanted the sum on the paper afterwards, because the clerk who did it is standing at the counter.

A house doesn't tear up its books because a woman off a rock has a better way. The head clerk asked where I got it. I told her the truth. She said, well. Then she gave me a dry coat. I'm not sour. If a stranger came to me with a better way to cut a press, I'd want to watch her cut one first.

Aud goes down to Kell in the spring. The table-house has taken her on to cut presses. I taught her that, at this bench, and it pays better there than it does here. She copied for me the whole of my watch and never once said the work was worth doing. She took her own sheets with her, which are hers, and I did not ask what was on them.

I'll put down one thing, for whoever is holding this book in a hundred passes. It was better. I offered it. They didn't want it.
