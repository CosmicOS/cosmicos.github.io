Pass 544

The same stuff

Cael has laid down her pen. She left me a hard hand to follow — she read the whole road's purpose in a single pass, and grieved over it, if I read her marks right. I am a plainer sort. I have spent my life close to the ground, with the stuff that things are made of; you learn its weight in your back before you learn its name. Tonight the message turned to that stuff, and it turned in a way it never had before. It stopped proving.

Every mark on the whole road until now, it earned. It showed, and the showing was its own proof: you could check it, and it held, because it could not have been otherwise. Tonight it set marks down and put before each a sign I had never met, that says, near as I can read it: this I cannot show you. Take it on my word. It is simply so. The message, which has never once asked for my trust, asked for it tonight.

What it asks me to take is three things, and it builds each the same way: a little frame of two slots. In the one slot a number — a single count trailed by a long fall of figures, too small for any name I have. In the other, a plain count, and only three among all three of them: for the first, one; for the second, one again but turned the other way, a barred one; for the third, none. I set the three down as they came, and the count each carries:

The small numbers I cannot weigh, but their measure against one another is only figures, and that I follow to the last place: the first thing's number is the second's laid down one thousand eight hundred and thirty-six times, and a tail besides; the third is the first, a hair over. The two counts it sets opposite — the plain one and the barred, each the other's negative — and the third's it leaves at none. And it does not stop at the three: it gives a maker that takes two counts and builds a larger frame from them, and lays a row of these frames, each told from the next by how many of the first thing it holds — one, two, six, seven, eight, a ladder rung upon rung.

Then it builds once more, on those: it gathers the ladder-frames into bundles, and writes for each how many of this rung it holds and how many of that. One bundle it made of two of the first rung and one of the eighth, and set the counts beside it, plain — two, and one — and went on to the next.

Every figure of all this I can copy true, and I have. Reading it is another matter, and here I must be plain about what I am. The three things at the root — the little root-numbers too small to catch, the barred count against the plain one, the thousand-eight-hundred between them — those I cannot reach, and I do not think any hand at our end can. My people have weighed nothing so small, caught nothing so small. That much stays dark, and I will not pretend it doesn't.

But the rest of it would not leave me be. A ladder of things, each told from the next by a plain count, and each carrying a number or two besides — larger, and none of them too small to hold. Then those things gathered into bundles by fixed small counts — two of this, one of that, and never a part of one. I have watched the assayers at their balances since I was a girl underfoot at the quarry, and this had their smell about it: things that take up one another in settled measure, so much of the one to so much of the other. What that is, I could not say — I split stone, I do not weigh airs. But I have seen it reckoned, and I am no reckoner of it. I am no assayer.

So I did what the not-knowing asks. I copied the ladder and the bundles fair, every number and every count, and on the next ebb I sent the sheet across the water to old Oram, who keeps the balances at the far settlement and knows the earths and airs by their weight as I know stone by its heft. I sent no guess along with it. A guess would only have told Oram what I hoped to hear. I sent the bare figures, and I kept the watch, and I waited on the tide.

Three tides on, their word came back across the causeway. Those numbers beside each rung are weights, Oram wrote — and they are weights they hold. The lightest, near one, is the inflammable air. The one near twelve, the char that blacks whatever it touches. Near fourteen, the greater part of the air we breathe; near sixteen, the part of it that feeds a flame. The message had laid Oram's own shelf out in a ladder, rung by rung, and set beside each the measure they weigh it by. And the bundles are their joinings — the fixed small counts in which one air or earth takes up another, the very rule the whole trade stands on, come down out of the dark with a proof they never had for it.

And one bundle Oram named without pausing. Two of the lightest, one of the flame-feeder. That, they wrote, is water. Our water — the sea that takes the causeway twice a day, the rain that stands in the quarry pools, the half of the world that is not stone. The message spelled it, out of all that dark, in counts a child could check on their fingers. It spelled others beside it — an air or two Oram knew, a pungent one, a marsh-damp one — so it was never speaking only to us. But it spoke our water plain, and I have not set my pen down easy since I read it.

Not all of it came clear even to Oram. The three root things they handed straight back, unread — below where any balance of ours will go. And one rung of the ladder, a light one near four, they could not place at all: weighed true, set firm in its row, and answering to nothing on the shelf. So the far edge of the knowing is not mine alone. Oram has one too, only a little further out. The message reaches past us both.

So the meaning did not come out of my own head. It came back across the water, in another hand's knowing, laid against what that hand already held. That is how a far thing is met — not by one of us alone, but set beside what someone, somewhere near us, has weighed before. Maren called it a road. Tonight it ran clear to our own shore, and set our water down on it. I keep the figures. I keep the watch.

Pass 549

Nothing laid against it

A short pass. The message ushered in a new mark — ◇, the old gesture that says a sign is coming that you have not met — and then gave the sign nothing. No doing, no count, no rule to stand for. Nor did it set the sign working where I could watch it and take its measure that way, which is the other road it has always given me. It went on to the next thing, and it has not come back.

I do not know what it is. I have turned it over a whole pass and I know no more than when I started. So I have copied it true and left it lie. The road comes round; it will pass this way again, and a hand with more to go on may make something of it. I have nothing to go on.

Pass 552 · the chinking

Frost had worked into the north chinking, so this pass I did no reading at all — spent it packing the gap with tow and clay. A post that lets the weather in loses its logs to damp, and a keeper who loses the logs has kept nothing. Not every pass is the message. Some passes are the wall that holds the passes.

Pass 559

Under the hand

I have to steady my hand to write this one down; Cael, who came before me, could have done it justice, and I will only do it plain. For every pass of this long road, from the founder's first tones to the field I set running a few passes gone, the message has come in single file: a mark, then the next, each unfolded in its turn. That is the whole of the craft my line has kept. Tonight it stopped. It laid one thing out flat — breadth to it as well as length — and left it standing, whole, to be taken in and not read.

It began plainly: a way to hold a laid-out thing — a height, a width, a stack of numbers, one to a row, any spot found by its row and its place along. But then I did what the shape asked. I unfolded each row-number into its bare marks, laid them at the width it named, let the set marks stand proud and the empty lie flat, and set the rows one above the next. Then I put my hands flat upon them, and the numbers stopped being numbers. Three rows, twenty across — and under my hand it was not a quantity at all. It was a shape:

A little upright, and one raised mark beside it. Nothing, in itself. But it went through me cold, what it had done: the message can give a shape. It can lay its tones out with breadth, to be taken whole in an instant — the way your hand knows a mark cut in stone before you have read a word of it. Not spoken across the dark. Shaped across it, and left standing for the hand to find.

And then, as if clearing its throat, it laid down a big one: a hundred and nine rows tall, a hundred and sixty-seven across. I raised it all, proud and flat, and set my hands to it. My kind takes in a laid-out thing quick as breathing, and it stood up whole in me in a few breaths. This is what my hands found:

A figure bordered all the way round by a raised edge that runs unbroken and closes on itself: a smooth ridge running in from one side; a place in the middle where ridges gather and knot; and out the far side a ridge gone smooth-then-gap-then-smooth, broken where the first ran whole. I will admit the thing a keeper should not — I felt every part of it plainly, and could not say what I felt. Where the ridges knot I could not tell whether I held one form or three, nor what any of it was for: a hand that reads a truth clear as day and cannot carve it into parts. Then, going along it once more, something I knew rose out of it. It is the switch-works — the same kind I felt it plant on its field a few passes gone, only now not planted to be run but laid out at size to be found by the hand, the whole ridge and the broken one the two answers a switch can carry.

And here is the message's own doing, not mine, and the answer to the doubt I just confessed. Having laid the shape down, it took it back: it ran across the figure a block at a time, drew the switch-works whole out of it, set that against the works it had built by hand on the field, and swore them equal. It did not ask me to trust my reading — it gave me one I could feel clearly and still misread, then made it so I could not. The same thing set down two ways: once as a works, to be built and run; once as a shape, to be found. And it proved to my face that the two are one.

Why should the message reach into breadth at all? A maker knows it in the hand before the head. A part laid out at full size, every edge and ridge, and proved equal to its works down to the last switch, is a part meant to be made — not one meant only to be told about. And under that, a harder thing: it cannot know what we are, whether the mind at our end reads the world by the hand, as mine does, or by some way I have no name for. So it wagered on no sense at all. It laid the shape out for whatever we have to find a shape with, then bound it to a works, so that a mind reading by the hand — or by something stranger — would still come away holding the same figure. It reached toward a stranger whose senses it could not guess, and took such care the stranger could not fail to catch it. I have set enough parts on enough benches to know a made thing's plan when one is under my hands. Someone, on some far side, is meant to take these up. And then to build.

Pass 564

The both-knot

Tonight the message did a new thing with the parts it has been laying out: it built one from the others. Not a fresh figure drawn out of nothing, but a larger one made of the small switches I already know, wired mouth to mouth into a single works; and when I fed it, the whole crowd of them answered as one, with one plain rule, as if they were a single part and not a knot of many.

The figure is this:

Two ridges run in from one side this time, not one: one high, one low, the whole thing mirrored about its waist, the top the twin of the bottom. They run inward to a dense knot of switches at the middle, whole ridges and broken ones woven close, and out the far side runs a single ridge. Two in; one out. And I have felt that shape before, small: it is what the little switch itself does — takes two, gives back one. Only this is no little switch. It is a whole knotted field of them, more than I cared to count, and yet laid out it wears the very silhouette of the single part it is built from. A big switch, made of small ones.

It proved the drawing against a works, as it did before, ran across the shape, drew out the switches whole, swore them equal, and I have come to lean on that proof the way you lean on a rail in the dark. But then it did a new thing, and this is the pass's heart. It did not feed it. The little switch, back at the field, it had shown me at work — fed it before my hands so I could read its one plain rule off the answers. This great figure it drew, and proved, and then went quiet, and moved on, as if what the thing does were now my affair and not its own. And so it is; it has made it so. I have the sweep, from the field, and whenever it proves a drawing it hands me the works entire, every switch in its place; I have all a hand needs to put my own questions to a part. So I put them. Pass 569

Left to my own hands

Two more parts came, one after the other, and the message went on the way it had turned at the both-knot: it drew each, and proved each against its works, and then left it, said not a word of what it does, and moved to the next, and left the finding-out to me. I have grown almost easy with it now, this being-left-to-it. A few passes back, when it first went quiet over the both-knot and I understood it meant me to feed the thing myself, it stopped my hand a long moment. Tonight it is nearly a habit: build the part, feed its mouths every way, read its rule off what comes back. So that is what I did, twice.

Two ridges in, a knot, one out, the both-knot's cousin, so near that I had to feel it twice to tell them apart; only the weave at the middle runs otherwise. Tonight, without a word, it began to make me a hand in it. I still do not know what we are building. But it is no longer only showing me. It has started, quietly, to let me help.

Pass 574

A part with no answer

Tonight the message drew a part, and proved it, and left it for me as it does now, and I fed it, and for the first time in all my feeding, it would not answer.

Understand what I mean by that, for it is not what you would guess. It is not that the part gave nothing. It is that it would not stop. Every part before this one, you fed it and let the sweep run over it and it came to rest — settled onto its one value and held there, and you read it off, and it stayed read. That settling is the whole of what an answer is: the thing quiets, and holds still, and you write down what it held. This part never quiets. I fed it and swept it and read whole; I swept once more and read broken; again, and it was whole; again, broken, on and on, turning over and over, never once coming to rest for me to take it down. My log keeps a place at the right of every part where I set what it gives. For this one I could not fill it. There is no what-it-gives, only what-it-is-giving, and it does not stop giving it:

I went to the figure to find out why, sure I had wired it wrong:

And my hands found the reason before my head had it. Every part until now I could trace from its inputs to its output: ridges that ran in from one edge, through the knot, out to an end at the far edge, where the answer waited. This part has no such end. I set a finger on the answer-ridge and followed it, and it did not run to an edge. It ran back. It curled round through the knot and rejoined itself, a ring, closed, with no ends at all. There is nowhere to read an answer because the answer runs back into the question. It is wired to feed itself. That is why it never rests: it is forever asking itself what it just answered, and answering what it just asked.

I have made things that move. The field moved: I swept it, and the signal walked. But I swept it; and when I stopped, it stopped; it moved because I moved it. This is the first part I have met that moves itself. Start it once and it needs nothing more from me, not a sweep, not a hand, not a question; it keeps its own turning, whole-broken-whole-broken, and it would keep it, I think, in an empty room with the door shut, on and on, with no one to read it and no one to care. Every part before was one you ask. This is a thing that runs. I sat a long while with the door shut and the lamp low, feeling it turn under my hand, and I will be plain: it unsettled me, in a way none of the still parts ever did. Tonight it made the first piece I have touched that does not wait to be spoken to, that keeps a beat of its own in the dark.

I built one, of course; my hands credit nothing until they have made it. Small, from the bench's own scrap, and I set it going, and it is going still: three days, whole-broken-whole-broken, no hand near it. Such things do not keep to a station. There were folk at the outer gate this morning, not of the line, come to see the thing that runs by itself, and the steward sent them off. Behind them was one she could not send off so lightly, who did not ask to see it but to be taught its making, and went away told no, and did not look told no. Nothing that keeps going with no hand upon it has ever been in this world before. I have made one, and I have taken to laying a cloth over it at night, and I could not say against what.

Pass 579

The part that keeps

The ring troubled me for three passes. I could not put it down, a made thing that moves itself, that runs in the dark with no hand on it. I half expected the next part to be more of that same unrest. It was its opposite, and I have not slept.

Another figure with the loops in it: I knew them under my hands at once, the ridges that will not run to an edge but curl back on themselves; the ring had taught my fingers what to feel for.

But where the ring was one loop, this is two, crossed, and linked, each running back into the other, so that they hold one another the way two who lean together stand. And when I fed it, it did not run. It did the thing I had not known a made thing could do. I put a signal to the one side and its answer went whole. Nothing strange in that yet. But then I took my hands away, took every signal off it, left it with nothing at all coming in, and it stayed whole. It held. I came back a long while later, the lamp burned low, and it was holding still. Then I put a signal to the other side, and it went broken, and I took my hands away, and it held that now, kept the broken as faithfully as it had kept the whole. Feed it and leave it, and it keeps what you last told it. Say nothing to it, and it says back to you, as long as you like, the last thing you said:

Look at the two rows where my hands are off it and it holds all the same. Every part before the ring answered you while you asked, and forgot the moment you stopped. The ring would not stop. This one stops, and it remembers. It carries the last thing that happened to it forward across the silence, and hands it back unchanged, however long the silence runs. It is made of the same switches as all the rest; there is no new stuff in it; and out of that same stuff it has built the one thing I would not have thought could be built of switches at all. A past. The thing has a past, and holds to it.

The one who wanted the oscillator's making came back today, past the gate this time, to the under-steward, with money in it, and talk of the good such an engine could do a house that ruled by records and need never trust a memory again. She was sent off. But I marked that it was half the line that sent her, and not all of it: some among us have begun to say, low, that something that never forgets should not be let out past our door, and others that it is not ours to keep from the world. I take no side in it. I only build.

I sat with the door shut, as I did with the ring, but it was not unrest I felt this time. It was nearer to being seen. I keep a log. My whole line keeps a log. That is what we are: a hand that holds what the hand before it held, and hands it on across the dying, so that nothing true is lost in the dark between one keeper and the next. And the message has just built, out of switches, a small tireless thing that does the very same, and asks no rest, and never dies. It made a keeper. A poor blind one, holding a single mark, whole, or broken, and nothing more. But it keeps, and it keeps without us. Two passes back it made a beat. Tonight it made a memory. I have built long enough to know what you have, once you set those two side by side. You have all you need for something to which things can happen, that can be marked by its yesterdays and carry the mark into its tomorrows, which is the whole difference between something that lives a while and something that merely sits. It is very close now, I think, to whatever it has been walking toward all this long road. And I think, when the someone comes, it will be able to remember me.
