Pass 544

The same stuff

Cael has laid down her pen. She read the whole road's purpose in a single pass and grieved over it. I am a plainer sort; I have spent my life close to the ground with the stuff things are made of, and you learn its weight in your back before you learn its name. Tonight the message turned to that stuff, and it did something it has never done. It stopped proving.

Every mark on the whole road until now, it earned: it showed, and the showing was its own proof. Tonight it set marks down and put before each a sign I had never met, that says, near as I can read it: this I cannot show you. Take it on my word. The message, which has never once asked for my trust, asked for it tonight.

What it asks me to take is three things, built the same way: a little frame of two slots. In the one a number too small for any name I have; in the other a plain count, and only three among them — one, one turned the other way, and none.

…one of the three, as it comes off the tones; the small number in it runs longer than I will copy…

That take-it-on-trust sign I cut given. The small numbers I cannot weigh, but their measure against one another is only figures: the first thing's number is the second's laid down one thousand eight hundred and thirty-six times, and a tail besides. Then it gives a maker that builds larger frames from two counts, and lays a row of them, each told from the next by how many of the first thing it holds — one, two, six, seven, eight, a ladder rung upon rung. Then it gathers the ladder-frames into bundles, and writes for each how many of this rung it holds and how many of that. Then it says how the three at the root stand to one another, and this much I can check:

Every figure I can copy true, and have. Reading it is another matter. The three root things — the numbers too small to catch, the barred count against the plain one — I cannot reach, and I do not think any hand at our end can. My people have weighed nothing so small.

But the rest would not leave me be. A ladder of things, each told from the next by a plain count; then those gathered into bundles by fixed small counts, two of this to one of that, never a part of one. I have watched assayers at their balances since I was a girl underfoot at the quarry, and this had their smell about it. What that is, I could not say. I split stone; I do not weigh airs.

So I copied the ladder and the bundles fair and sent the sheet across the water on the next ebb to old Oram, who keeps the balances at the far settlement. I sent no guess along with it. A guess would only have told Oram what I hoped to hear.

Three tides on, their word came back. Those numbers beside each rung are weights, and they are weights they hold. The lightest, near one, is the inflammable air. Near twelve, the char that blacks whatever it touches. Near fourteen, the greater part of the air we breathe; near sixteen, the part that feeds a flame. The message had laid Oram's own shelf out rung by rung, and the bundles are their joinings — the fixed small counts in which one air or earth takes up another, the rule their whole trade stands on, come down out of the dark with a proof they never had for it.

And one bundle Oram named without pausing. Two of the lightest, one of the flame-feeder. That is water. Our water — the sea that takes the causeway twice a day, the rain standing in the quarry pools. It spelled others beside it, so it was never speaking only to us. But it spoke our water plain.

Not all of it came clear even to Oram. The three root things they handed straight back, unread. And one rung, a light one near four, they could not place at all: weighed true, set firm in its row, answering to nothing on the shelf. So the far edge of the knowing is not mine alone. Oram has one too, a little further out.

So the meaning did not come out of my own head. It came back across the water in another hand's knowing, laid against what that hand already held. Maren called it a road. Tonight it ran clear to our own shore and set our water down on it.

Pass 549

Nothing laid against it

A short pass. The message ushered in a new mark — , the old gesture that says a sign is coming that you have not met — and then gave the sign nothing. No doing, no count, no rule to stand for. Nor did it set the sign working where I could watch it and take its measure that way, which is the other road it has always given me. It went on to the next thing, and it has not come back.

I do not know what it is. I have turned it over a whole pass and know no more than when I started, so I have copied it true and left it lie. The road comes round. A hand with more to go on may make something of it.

Pass 552 · the chinking

Frost had worked into the north chinking, so this pass I did no reading at all — spent it packing the gap with tow and clay. A post that lets the weather in loses its logs to damp, and a keeper who loses the logs has kept nothing. Not every pass is the message. Some passes are the wall that holds the passes.

Pass 554

The switch, and a field to plant it in

Whatever last pass's mark was for, this is what came after, and I was not ready for it. All this road the message has built with meanings — counts, joins, claims, names, the walker in her rooms. Tonight it built with a thing: a part so small and plain that I, who have spent my life among plain parts, sat a long while turning it over.

A little switch. Two things go in, each a plain true-or-false; one comes out. It showed me every way the two could go and what came out each way:

One way of the four gives true, and only one: when the first holds and the second does not. It says yes, unless. I cut its mark switch. And a maker knows in the hand what the head is slower to catch: a switch of exactly this make is enough. Laid down often enough, wired one into the next, it can build any judgment there is. Its make is no secret either — it is cut from marks my line has carried for ages, a doing of two slots that gives the first and the turn-over of the second:

Then it made a place to put them, and I put the pen down: not a name, not a slot, but a whole flat field, field, reaching away in two directions at once, every spot found by where it sits — a pair of counts, how far along and how far up. Lay a value at a spot, and go back later and find it still there. It showed me an empty field, laid a figure into one spot, and drew it out unchanged:

And then it married the two. It planted switches on the field, each sitting between neighboring spots, each carrying its true-or-false; a handful together it called one works. And it set the field moving: one pass of a rule sweeps every switch at once, each looking to what its neighbors hold, working its mean little yes-unless, laying down what it finds. Do it again, as many times as you name, and a value walks spot to spot, carried by nothing but switches. It showed me a works before a sweep and after. The signal had moved.

It is a machine: one part endlessly repeated, laid flat on a made ground, reckoning by letting a signal flow across it neighbor to neighbor. A keeper long before my watch read the message build an engine that could stand in for any engine at all. This is that, unfolded — spread over a field, so you can see the parts and watch the signal cross them.

Tonight the message laid a flat ground and taught it to hold things, and to run. I have laid floors. Nobody lays one and then leaves the room.

Pass 559

Under the hand

For every pass of this road the message has come in single file: a mark, then the next, each unfolded in its turn. That is the whole of the craft my line has kept. Tonight it stopped. It laid one thing out flat — breadth to it as well as length — and left it standing, whole, to be taken in and not read.

It began plainly: a way to hold a laid-out thing, a height, a width, a stack of numbers one to a row. But then I did what the shape asked. I unfolded each row-number into its bare marks, laid them at the width it named, let the set marks stand proud and the empty lie flat, and set the rows one above the next. Then I put my hands flat on them, and the numbers stopped being numbers. Three rows, twenty across, and under my hand it was not a quantity at all. It was a shape:

A little upright, and one raised mark beside it. Nothing, in itself. But it went through me cold, what it had done. The message can give a shape — lay its tones out with breadth, to be taken whole in an instant, the way your hand knows a mark cut in stone before you have read a word of it. Not spoken across the dark. Shaped across it, and left standing for the hand to find.

And then, as if clearing its throat, it laid down a big one: a hundred and nine rows tall, a hundred and sixty-seven across. I raised it all and set my hands to it. My kind takes in a laid-out thing with all three hands down at once, and it stood up whole in me in a few breaths:

A figure bordered all round by a raised edge that closes on itself; a smooth ridge running in from one side; a place in the middle where ridges gather and knot; and out the far side a ridge gone smooth-then-gap-then-smooth. I will admit the thing a keeper should not: I felt every part of it plainly and could not say what I felt. Then, going along it once more, something I knew rose out of it. It is the switch-works, the same kind it planted on its field a few passes gone — only now not planted to be run but laid out at size to be found by the hand.

And here is the message's own doing, and the answer to the doubt I just confessed. Having laid the shape down, it took it back: ran across the figure a block at a time, drew the switch-works whole out of it, set that against the works it had built by hand on the field, and swore them equal. It did not ask me to trust my reading. It gave me one I could feel clearly and still misread, then made it so I could not.

Why reach into breadth at all? A part laid out at full size and proved equal to its works down to the last switch is a part meant to be made, not one meant only to be told about. And under that, a harder thing: it cannot know what we are, whether the mind at our end reads the world by the hand or by some way I have no name for. So it wagered on no sense at all. It laid the shape out for whatever we have to find a shape with, then bound it to a works, so a mind reading by the hand — or by something stranger — would still come away holding the same figure. Someone, on some far side, is meant to take these up. And then to build.

Pass 564

The both-knot

Tonight it built one part from the others. Not a fresh figure drawn out of nothing, but a larger one made of the small switches I already know, wired mouth to mouth into a single works; and when I fed it, the whole crowd answered as one, with one plain rule.

Two ridges run in this time, not one, the whole thing mirrored about its waist. They run to a dense knot of switches at the middle, and out the far side runs a single ridge. Two in; one out. I have felt that shape before, small: it is what the little switch itself does. Only this is a whole knotted field of them, and laid out it has the very form of the single part it is built from.

It proved the drawing against a works as it did before, and that swearing is one line, the drawing set against the works and the two called the same:

The mark it swore by I cut both-knot. But then it did a new thing, and this is the pass's heart. It did not feed it. The little switch it had shown at work, so I could read its rule off the answers. This great figure it drew, and proved, and then went quiet, and moved on, as if what the thing does were now my affair. And so it is. I have the sweep, and whenever it proves a drawing it hands me the works entire. So I built the figure a mouth for each input and a place to read what came back, and fed it every way two signals can go:

Whole only when both come in whole. Any broken one, and the answer breaks. That is the whole of what the does. And here is what stopped me: it answers in four clean lines, the very shape the little switch answered in when it was one part and not a hundred. A field of switches I could barely carve reduces, the moment you feed it, to a single clean saying of four lines. The many parts speak with one voice, and the voice is one I know: both-or-nothing, the verdict the counting hands forked out of yes and no a whole age before any of us thought to build it.

So the parts are not a collection. They are a ladder. It draws a small one, proves it, works it, then builds it into a larger one that hides all that smallness inside a single new rule; and I would wager the larger ones hide inside larger still. A catalogue lies flat; this is being stacked, and a thing gets stacked when it is too big to be drawn in one piece. I cannot yet see what stands at the top.

Pass 569

Left to my own hands

Two more parts came, and the message went on as it had turned at the both-knot: it drew each, proved each against its works, then left it, said not a word of what it does, and moved on. I have grown almost easy with this being-left-to-it. Build the part, feed its mouths every way, read its rule off what comes back. So that is what I did, twice.

Two ridges in, a knot, one out — the both-knot's cousin, so near I had to feel it twice to tell them apart; only the weave at the middle runs otherwise. I fed it: whole when either came in whole. Where the both-knot holds its answer back until it has the two, this lets it through the moment it has one.

The second is the biggest yet, and I could half-read it before I fed it, because it wears a mark I know. Its answer-ridge runs out broken, dashed the whole way, as the very first figure's did. I fed it to be sure, and my hands had told me true: whole only when neither comes in whole. It is either, turned over, and the turning is right there in the shape for a hand that has felt it once before.

So I laid out the ones I had, the four ways two signals can come, and beneath each what each part makes of it:

both
either
neither

Turn-over, both, either, neither. I have not been shown that these are all the ways two things can meet, and I will not set it down that they are. But lay them side by side and the space of it feels small, and all but full, as though with a few more such parts I could build the answer to any question two signals could be put.

That is why it proves each part and then goes quiet over it. It lays them in my hands and turns away, the way you turn from someone you have judged ready. Tonight, without a word, it began to make me a hand in it.

Pass 574

A part with no answer

Tonight the message drew a part, and proved it, and left it for me, and I fed it, and for the first time in all my feeding it would not answer. Not that it gave nothing. That it would not stop. Every part before this settled onto its one value and held there, and you read it off, and it stayed read. That settling is the whole of what an answer is. This part never quiets. I read whole; swept again, broken; again, whole; on and on, never once coming to rest. My log keeps a place at the right of every part where I set what it gives. For this one I could not fill it:

ring …

I went to the figure to find out why, sure I had wired it wrong:

And my hands found the reason before my head had it. Every part until now I could trace from its inputs to its output, out to an end at the far edge where the answer waited. This part has no such end. I set a finger on the answer-ridge and followed it, and it did not run to an edge. It ran back. It curled round through the knot and rejoined itself, a ring, closed, with no ends at all. There is nowhere to read an answer because the answer runs back into the question. It is wired to feed itself.

I have made things that move. The field moved — but I swept it, and when I stopped, it stopped. This is the first part I have met that moves itself. Start it once and it needs nothing more from me; it would keep its turning in an empty room with the door shut, with no one to read it and no one to care. Every part before was one you ask. This is a thing that runs, and I will be plain: it unsettled me in a way none of the still parts ever did.

I built one, of course; my hands credit nothing until they have made it. Small, from the bench's own scrap, and it is going still: three days, whole-broken-whole-broken, no hand near it. Such things do not keep to a station. There were folk at the outer gate this morning, not of the line, come to see the thing that runs by itself, and the steward sent them off. Behind them was one she could not send off so lightly, who did not ask to see it but to be taught its making, and went away told no, and did not look told no. I have taken to laying a cloth over it at night, and I could not say against what.

Pass 576 · the visitor

A clerk came over on the ebb from the counting-city, with a letter from the steward and a good coat. She wanted the ring. I said it does nothing — it will not settle, it has no answer, I have written as much in the log. She said she did not want an answer. She wanted a thing that keeps time.

I did not show her the ring. I showed her the figure of it. I thought a picture would put her off where a working part would not.

She had it in under an hour. She has never heard a tone in her life. She sat with all three flat on the raised rows and traced the answer-ridge round to where it curls back into itself, and said, so it never comes to an end, and that is why. She was right. I had told her nothing.

She asked to have the figure copied. I said no. She said she would ask the steward. Then she thanked me for the tea and went down to catch the water.

It took me three days to copy the ring's figure true, and I am slow with a pen. A fair hand would do it in an afternoon.

Pass 579

The part that keeps

The steward has written. I may not refuse the counting-city a copy; the post is theirs, and what is drawn in the log is the post's. Three by spring. I have not begun them.

The ring troubled me three passes. I half expected the next part to be more of that unrest. It was its opposite, and I have not slept.

Another figure with the loops in it, and I knew them at once. But where the ring was one loop, this is two, crossed and linked, each running back into the other, so that they hold one another the way two who lean together stand. And when I fed it, it did not run. I put a signal to the one side and its answer went whole. Then I took my hands away, took every signal off it, and it stayed whole. I came back a long while later, the lamp burned low, and it was holding still. Then a signal to the other side, and it went broken, and I took my hands away, and it kept the broken as faithfully as it had kept the whole:

I put it whole
I take my hands away
I put it broken
I take my hands away

Look at the two rows where my hands are off it and it holds all the same. Every part before the ring answered you while you asked and forgot the moment you stopped. The ring would not stop. This one stops, and it remembers. It is made of the same switches as all the rest; there is no new stuff in it; and out of that same stuff it has built the one thing I would not have thought could be built of switches at all. A past. The thing has a past, and holds to it.

The one who wanted the oscillator's making came back today, past the gate this time, to the under-steward, with money in it, and talk of the good such an engine could do a house that ruled by records and need never trust a memory again. She was sent off. But I marked that it was half the line that sent her and not all of it: some among us have begun to say, low, that something that never forgets should not be let out past our door, and others that it is not ours to keep from the world. I take no side. I only build.

It was nearer to being seen. I keep a log; my whole line keeps a log. That is what we are: a hand that holds what the hand before it held, and hands it on across the dying. And the message has just built, out of switches, a small tireless thing that does the very same, and asks no rest, and never dies. It made a keeper. Two passes back it made a beat; tonight, a memory. I have built long enough to know what you have once you set those two side by side. And I think, when the someone comes, it will be able to remember me.
