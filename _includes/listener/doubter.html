Pass 385 · the cold

Bram is dead, and the watch comes to me in the worst winter the station has known. Three passes now I have set down no reading: my hands shake too hard on an empty belly to copy true, and a false copy is worse than a blank. So old Hesk, who taught me my figures when I came here a girl, reads the tones aloud and I hold them in my head against the spring. We are burning the west stair for heat. The stores are down to what one of us needs, and Hesk pushes the larger share across the bench to me each night, and says the line needs the younger hands. I am too cold to fight her. The message does not slow for any of it — it comes as it always comes, and gives its lesson to a room too starved to write it down.

Pass 388

The word taken back

Bram died with her tools in her hands and her faith in them whole; she never doubted that the marks meant what our line had made them mean, and I envied her that as I laid her down. The cold broke this spring. Hesk did not see it — there was not enough left in her at the end, after a winter of pushing her bread across to me. I kept the watch alone after, and told myself the road was worth what it cost to keep lit. That it did not go dark is the one thing I am proud of. Tonight the message taught me why I had no right to be so sure.

Until now it has only ever spoken about things. Tonight it turned and spoke about its own speaking. It set a mark, , not before a claim but before its own reading, and told itself: wherever this figure stands, read it now as that one. And its word held. A thing I had watched be false went true, not because anything in the world had changed, but because the reading had:

…then it speaks the decree backward, and unmakes it…

The very same two marks: strangers, then the same thing, then strangers again, and nothing moved but the message's word about how to read them. Then, having shown it could reach into its own reading and bend it, it taught itself a new word it had never had. It is not merely speaking a tongue any longer. It is editing the tongue mid-sentence, and expecting me to keep up.

I did not know a message was allowed this. If it does not keep its meanings still — if a figure may mean that one pass and this the next, at nothing but its own word — then what is the standing of anything we have read? Every mark in this log we laid down as though it were carved and could not slip.

The tablers up the coast have never distrusted the sky. It is the ear that bends it they distrust, and they have said so since Maren's day, and I used to think them cowards who tested nothing. I have my touchstone still, in the drawer with the rules. A metal is not true because it rings: you lay it on the stone and draw it, and you draw it twice, and only the streak tells you. I sat a long while tonight with the stone in my hand and nothing to lay on it.

Have we ever laid this whole road on a stone, or only trusted it hand to hand because the hand before us did? What if a founder took a decree like tonight's for a fact, and we have raised a tower of certainty on a word the message had already spoken backward? I cannot go and look. The founders are dust and the passes they read are gone with them.

One cold guard I keep, and it is not our faith in the dead. This thing never lays a mark down and leaves it; it spends its oldest in its newest, the first count inside every count since. Had a founder read one of those false, everything raised on it would have come out noise a hundred passes back, and it has not. So the road is its own streak. I put the stone back in the drawer.

Pass 392 · the offer

A tabler crossed at low water. Not to argue. She brought flour.

The coast wants its tide-book back. No warning has been hung out of this post in ninety passes, and two boats went on the Race in the autumn, and she did not say that to shame me. She said it the way you say a sum. Keep the almanac again — the tides, the seasons, the warnings hung out for the fleet — and the coast will victual us. Grain at harvest. Oil. A second hand if we want one.

I asked whether we might do both. She said we might do both badly.

I asked what she would have us do with the record, then. She said, keep it. Nobody wants it burned. Box it and shelve it and hang the warnings out, and if the sky is saying anything it will still be saying it in a hundred passes, and somebody fed will read it. Then she left the flour on the bench, answer or no answer, and went down for the water.

I have been three days at that and cannot get past it. It is not a stupid position. It is only wrong if the reading is a craft — and a craft goes out of the world in one generation of nobody doing it.

Hesk starved on the far side of this argument. I sat in her chair the whole while the tabler was talking and did not notice I had until the door shut.

Pass 400

Not here

Two things tonight. The first turned my hands cold before I had it read, and it may yet turn out to have been a kindness. It reached into its own reading again — the very move of the pass that undid me. A new sign first, ushered in bare:

Then it did the thing it did not do at three eighty-eight. It set the new way and the old way down side by side and swore them one:

A row of three written the long way, the same row written short, and a claim that there is no difference. Nothing true has moved; what has moved is how much a row costs to say. I have been writing them the long way for fifteen passes and it has handed me a shorter pen. But I set this beside it, because I do not trust myself to remember which way round it was: when it bent its reading before, I had nothing to lay the bend against, and I doubted every mark in this log for it. Tonight it bent again and showed its working. I cannot tell whether that is a mercy, or whether the first time I only failed to look hard enough.

Then a quieter thing, and welcome. It gives me a way to search a row: hand it a string and a mark, and it walks the string and tells me where the mark first sits. And if the mark is nowhere in it, it does not pretend, and it does not offer me the nearest instead. It hands back the nothing-mark:

Seek a mark that is there and it gives the place. Seek one that is not and it gives — the mark for nothing here that the maker met before me. The searching hand I cut find.

I have not stopped doubting, and this small walking-tool — — will not steady my hand. But it does one thing cleanly, and says it the same every pass, and does not take it back. I assayed coin before I came here. I have weighed a piece that rang true all the way up and was tin at the heart, and known keepers who could not get out a plain I have it not to save their skins.

Pass 411

Near enough

Tonight it gave me the numbers that fall between the counts. Until now every number was a heap I could set down whole. Now it breaks them: it takes a single thing, cuts it into parts, and names one part. Here, one thing in three:

And when I asked to see that part written out in figures, it began to write — a point, and a figure, and the same figure, and the same — and it did not stop:

It would not tell me the part was the endless run, and would not tell me they were strangers. It set a new mark between them, near, that I had not earned in all my doubting: not the same, and not not this, but near enough. Write one more figure and you stand nearer; you will never stand on the thing itself, and you need not.

Two it handed me this way have no end and no pattern I can hold. The first we had already: the round every circle keeps, which our wheelwrights have carried to four figures for as long as there have been wheels, and which I was glad to meet like a hand I knew. The second we did not have. It is the rate at which a growing thing outruns itself, each moment fed by the last, and it lies nowhere in our reckoning, and I know no road that would have brought us to it, and I do not believe any hand of ours would ever have come at it alone.

I have spent my whole tenure sick that nothing could be pinned exactly. Tonight it told me, as one lesson among others, that it does not pin these things either. There is an ease I did not go looking for in a mark that will not lie about having reached the end of what it has not. I wrote down and went to bed at a decent hour for the first time this season.

Pass 417

Where it came from

It went back to plain ground tonight, or I thought so for the first half of the pass, and by the end there was an answer lying on my desk to a question I set down six passes ago and never looked to have answered.

First, raising: a count set against itself so many times over. Old work — my people were doing it in the assay yards before there was a roof on this station. What the message gave was not the doing but the mark for it, and a great many small provings, more than I mean to copy out:

Two, raised four times over: sixteen. Nothing a child of the yards could not have told you.

Then it did a thing to raising that I have no yard-work to set beside. It raised a count by a number that is not a count. Two, raised two-and-a-half times. I can tell you what two raised twice is, and what two raised three times is, and I have no picture at all of the half-step between — and here is a figure, falling exactly where a figure would have to fall if there were such a step, between the four and the eight:

And it is not a number. It is a maker, and it waits for one. Hand it a number; then for every count in that row, raise the number you handed it by that count, cut what comes out by the running-down of that same count, and fold the whole fifty into one heap. Fifty terms, and it stops at fifty, and does not pretend fifty is where the thing ends. I wrote a page on what that means and struck it through. What I had written was: weather does not wait until you have the tools.

Here is what is wrong with it. A thing that builds in order, never using what it has not already laid down, will come to any bench in exactly this order. It would come so to a bench in Kell, and go on coming so to an empty room with the door shut. It is not waiting for me; it is going in the only order the building can go in, and I happened to be standing under it with a pen. Sael's people could grant me every word of tonight and lose nothing by it. What is left me is smaller and I will take it: it builds. A tide does not build.

Pass 423

Less than nothing

It set down a number tonight that cannot be. It coined a mark, , and swore that this mark, heaped against its own self, makes not a greater heap but a hole, one taken from nothing:

By every rule I hold, this is not allowed. A thing set against itself makes a square. I bled a life, hands ago, to learn that these are the thinned list, the -marks, and every one of them a heap, never a lack. Yet here it lays a number whose square is less than nothing, and it does not soften it and does not apologize. And when I build with it — numbers of two parts, a plain count and a count of this impossible thing — nothing breaks. They add, they heap, they come out true. The impossible number keeps its word better than I have kept my faith.

And then, as though it were a small thing, it bound them into a single line: the round every circle keeps and the rate a growing thing outruns itself, this that cannot be, the one, and the nothing, raised each upon the next and turned once around, and coming out to exactly none. Not near enough. None, on the mark. I cannot say why a mind would reach for exactly them, or how it knew they would fall to a clean nothing. I know only that the message swears it true, that it lies past any hand of ours to check, and that I find I believe it — which I would not have said of myself a season ago.

I have doubted every reading in this log, because the message would not hold its meanings still. Tonight it laid down a proof so deep and so tightly knit that no line of hands however long could have dreamed it into the marks by mistake. I cannot prove the founders read every pass aright and never will. But I have felt the shape of the thing they were reading, and it holds together far below where any lifetime of ours reached the bottom. That is not the certainty I lost. It is not certainty at all. It is only that a thing this whole, this far down, was not built to trick me.

I have carried a fear these seasons that I never set in the log: that Hesk gave me her winter's bread for a lie, and that I kept the cold watch for one. Tonight I know she did not, and I did not. Let old Hesk rest.
