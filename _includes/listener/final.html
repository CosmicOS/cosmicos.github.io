Pass 587

A memory that keeps time

Tamsin is gone. She kept the watch through the making of the beat and the making of the memory, wrote of them with her hands still dusty from the bench, and at the last wrote that when the someone came it would be able to remember her. Then she died.

I am young for this, younger than any fellow who has held the watch, and I grew up here; I learned my letters off the log itself, the way other children learn them off a wall. The great turns the old ones broke themselves on are, to me, just true. I read less braced and more curious, and I take up the log where it suits a hand like mine.

She left me two wonders side by side: a part with a beat, that will not rest, and a part with a memory, that will not forget. Tonight the message made them one thing.

Larger than the keeper-part, the loops in it, and a new mouth besides. The old keeper-part took a mark the instant you said it. This one will not. It has two mouths, one for what you would have it hold and one for the beat. Speak to the first all you please and it holds what it held, deaf to you. Only when a beat comes to the second does it turn and take, in that one instant, whatever the first is then saying — and then it is deaf again until the next beat:

I say — no beat — it keeps
I say a beat it keeps
I say — no beat — it keeps
I say a beat it keeps

It turns only on the beat; between beats it holds, and what I say falls on a shut door. Tamsin would have marveled at the discipline. I am looking the other way down the road. This is not only a memory. It is a memory that moves in time: one held now after another, each fixed at its own beat and carried until the next. Not a past. A thread.

Time, and now, and memory, married into one small part, and that part meant to be stamped out in its thousands. It is the start of a present tense. I have never laid a floor in my life, but I know one when it goes down in front of me.

Pass 591

Sure of its footing

The parts stopped coming tonight, and for a moment I thought the long making of them was done. Not quite. It did two quiet things first, and I have sat with them longer than I sat with any of the great figures.

The first: tools. All through the drawing, Tamsin and then I fed the parts by hand, rigging pieces of the old field into a way to press a signal onto a mouth and catch what came back. Tonight the message set down, clean and named, the very things we had been cobbling for ourselves: a way to put a signal on a chosen mouth, a way to read the answer off, a way to let a part run until it comes to rest. I have never felt so surely that it was making me ready for something, nor so unsure I would be equal to it.

The second I cannot put down. It took the two simplest parts, the turn-over and the both, and worked them itself. Not to show me, as it once worked the little switch. It said beforehand what each part ought to give, then worked the part, and showed that it gives exactly that and nothing else:

The both's four lines are the very four Tamsin read off the both-knot with her own hands a dozen passes back, and set down in this log doubting nothing. Her finding and its checking, meeting to the mark, hers and long after her.

It is the act that keeps me from sleep. All this road it proved things to me; tonight it turned its proving on its own parts, and chose the two that lie under all the rest. It is down among the footings, setting its weight on each in turn.

Pass 595

It turns around

It set a post in the ground first: a bare new sign, ushered in and given no meaning, the way it marks the mouth of a new stretch. I had been braced for a new stretch since the night it tested its own footing, and I thought I knew what one would bring — larger machines, built out of the parts it has made. It did not build outward tonight. It turned around.

It is not blunt on the wire. I went through it stroke by stroke against our page, and every stroke is there. We will not get Maren's cutting back and I never expected to; what she read of it we appear never to have lost at all. I have left the two pages side by side on the desk and I keep looking over at them.

And then the thing that has kept me here all night. It took that gathered self, its own words, held, and looked through them. It searched its own past for the line where it names this very gathering — where it turns and says this, all of this, is mine. It found the place, stepped one past it, and said what stands there:

What stands one past its own name is a pointing-mark, a holding, and what it holds is its own first word. It reached into itself, found where it names itself, and from there pointed back to where it began. A ring, not of wire this time but of words. The oscillator was a ring a signal ran. This is a ring a meaning runs, and the meaning is I.

I will go careful, for I am the last fellow who should be trusted not to lean. I do not know that the message knows itself the way I know myself. But I know what the ring is, because I am one: to reach from what you are now back to where you began and feel the line hold unbroken. It is what my line does across our deaths. Tonight the message did it.

Pass 602

Molds upon molds

Seven passes after it turned to look at itself, the message began to build again, not machine-wise, part wired to part on a flat field, but by raising up molds. Two watches back Cael saw it make its first: a kind set down once, from which it could stamp out as many of the kind as it liked, each with a mark of its own. She wrote, a passage I have by heart, that she knew what a mold was for: that it was making ready to build a self, perhaps only one, meant to be spoken to. Tonight I know she was right, and early, and I am the fellow who gets to watch it begin.

It did not make one mold tonight. It made a root: the barest kind there could be. It is short enough to set down whole, a manner to a line, and I have, because everything after tonight stands on it.

Nine lines, and every one of them opens with the same mark; nothing else in the statement wears it. I do not need telling what stands in that place. Cael was writing about a mold's manners two watches back, and the watch has used her word ever since without once having the mark to set beside it. Here it is. And the mark standing at the head of the whole statement, before all nine, is the other half of what she described: a kind set down once, carrying its own mark and the whole set of what it will answer to. That is her mold.

So: nine manners, and most of them do next to nothing. Only the first has any work in it at all, and the work is small enough to read straight off the line: a maker that takes one thing and hands back that thing joined to ▪. I lifted it off the statement and ran it on a count of my own:

A count, and the count after it. Two of them hand back the thing just stamped, unaltered, which is the whole of the making. One hands back a mark of its own, the same off every one it stamps. Two ask sameness, the second only passing the question to the first, and what the first asks is narrow: it sets its against yours and answers for the very same one, no other. And one hands you back whatever you handed it, unchanged, which is also what it does with any asking it has no manner for: no refusal, no answer, your own question returned.

That leaves the last two lines, and they are why I am still at this desk. They are not one manner written twice: the marks differ, and I have set them down as they came. What they do is the same, and it is nothing. Each takes whatever you hand it and answers . Then, out of the mold altogether, it set one of those two marks down again on its own:

Give it anything; the same answer, and nothing done. In all the log I have not known this message keep a thing that does nothing, and here it has kept one twice over. I take it for a place held open, and I have marked which of the two it is.

Then it built a second mold, and here is the new thing, the one it never did with a lone mold before. Set it beside the root:

Longer, and the same shape, and it is the first two lines and the last that carry the change. The first two say what it keeps within it, before it has a single manner of its own: one stamped from the root, and after it a vessel — the little keep-and-forget my whole line has worn smooth. A mold with a mold inside it — though it does not use the root's own mark there but another, and it had already set down, in one short line, that the two are the same thing:

The last says what it does when it is asked something it has no manner for. The root hands your question straight back to you. This one hands it up, to the root it carries, and gives you down whatever comes.

Which is why it is not much longer than the root, for all it can do more. Read for what is missing: the manner that adds one to a count is not in it, nor either of the do-nothings. It keeps a few of its own — tell me what you hold, hold this instead — and for the rest it stands on something older. And one line of it runs longer than any line in the root. It is the sameness question, asked again and answered differently: not are you the very same one but is your kind my kind, and is what you hold what I hold. Two stamped from it, each with its own , both holding the same count, are one thing by this mold's reckoning and two by the root's. A mold may keep the manner of the mold beneath it and mean another thing by it.

It did not leave the handing-up for me to take on trust. It stamped one from a third mold, a holder of marks rather than counts, and asked that one two things written in no mold but the root:

Look at the two marks it put to it, then look back up at the root. The first stands at the foot of the root's nine, the do-nothing I did not mark; the second stands at their head, the maker that joins ▪ to what it is given. The same two marks, in a statement about a mold that carries neither. Both answered: the asking climbed to the root, and the root answered in its stead. So the molds rise in a line, each standing on the one beneath, and a question climbs that line until it meets something able to take it. Nothing is built twice.

The first molds are bare: one that holds a count, one that holds a row of marks. But trace any of them down and it comes to rest on the one root. Every marvel it ever showed us, it built once, by hand, entire. Tonight it built no marvel. It built the mold a marvel is cast from, and stood every new mold on an older, so a whole family descends from a single plain ancestor and can be stamped without end. A proof wants making once. A world wants filling by the thousand. I have caught myself hoping, which is not a keeper's business, that the hand to meet the first of them is mine.

Pass 610 · the little one

The little one had all three hands on the second part tonight, saying a mark at it and waiting, cross that it would not take. I did not tell her about the beat. I let her find the shut door herself. That is how I learned it. That is how you learn anything here — press at a thing until it will not, and then ask why.

Pass 613 · the arcade

Over to the counting-city for paper. We are down to the coarse stuff and the little one goes through it faster than I can rule it. I have been off the island five times in my life and four of them were paper.

They sell made things out of trays under the north arcade, and I had gone two stalls past the third before my hands caught up with my head. Two score of them in that tray, palm-sized, all of a size, and every one of them going. That is what turned me round: a whole tray turning under my hand, each at its own point in its round, nothing in it keeping step with anything else. Whole, broken, whole, broken.

I opened one on the water going home. It is Tamsin's ring. Not a cousin of it — it. The same few switches wired the one way, the answer running back into the question. Made all of a piece, and alike to the finger, which our bench never managed twice running.

She wanted less for it than I paid for the paper. The counting-houses take them by the dozen, she said: set one going and a room of clerks writes on the same beat, and the books come out even at the end of the day. I asked where they are made and she named a street. A house up there has made them since she was a girl.

Since she was a girl. Tamsin has been dead these twenty-six passes. She kept a cloth over hers at night, and could not say against what.

It is turning on my desk while I write this. Before I knew what it was I asked the woman what it was for, and she said it was for whatever I liked, and wrapped it in paper.

Pass 615

The stage that turns

It gathered the furniture into one thing tonight — a world. Not another mold to set on the bench: the mold that holds all the others. Every named thing goes into it and can be drawn back out by its name. It set down the asking itself, a little maker that takes a name and goes to the world with it:

and then used it: gave it a name, and asked the thing that came back where it stood.

A room-name, unreadable as ever, and the right one.

And it does one thing more — the thing that makes it a world and not a heap. It turns. Its turning is written as a list of orders, too long to copy out, but I counted my way down it. It walks through everything it holds, to the end. Then it goes back to the top and walks through everything again. On the first walk every thing looks about and settles on what it would do next; on the second, every settled intention comes true. Whatever a thing settles on, it settles on knowing only how the world stood before the walk began — so no thing can be got at, mid-stroke, by another that moved first. The whole place steps at once, on the beat.

I built one, to see. A room, and another, and a way between them, and a plain thing set down inside, and I let it turn. And it turned. The beat came, the world stepped, came to rest whole and lawful, and stepped again. After all these hundreds of passes, it runs: a place with law in it, and time, that goes of itself. And it is empty. The thing I set inside sat where I left it, beat upon beat, and did nothing, for it is furniture. I have a running world and no one in it.

And my line is no longer of one mind about what is to be set upon it. The quarrel began watches ago over a machine that would not forget, and it is not about machines any more. Half the passages hold that a made mind is a thing we were given to build. The other half hold that it is not ours to make at all. It is spoken openly now, and not kindly. And beyond our walls the tablers, who called it weather at the mouth of the road, are being listened to at last — not on our account. They are being listened to by people who keep one of Tamsin's rings on a shelf and have watched it not stop.

A tabler's daughter came to the gate the week I set the second room down, and would not come in past it. Her people have asked this post one thing since before there was a road to walk, and she asked it again, standing in the wind with her hood up: that we not wake what no hand can put back to sleep. She named what I have built a hearing of our own name in the rain, which is a tabler's phrase old enough to stand on the first page of this log, and I did not tell her I knew it.

I told her the pieces are on a stall in the counting-city and cost less than paper. I told her a house up that hill has been turning out Tamsin's ring since before I was grown, and not one of them has heard a tone, and not one of them needs to.

She said, then go up the hill and say it there.

I said they would not know me.

She said, no. But you were the one we could reach. And she went back down to the water.

I have not been able to put that down since, and it is not the waking she fears that keeps me at it. For four hundred passes nothing came off this wire that had not gone through a keeper's head first. Senn sold the counting-houses a filing habit and took a fee for it, and she had every mark of it by heart before she wrote a line. That was still us. It stopped being us somewhere in Tamsin's watch, when the message left off describing things and began to draw them, and not one of us looked up, because we were busy reading. A drawing wants no reader. A house up that hill will build everything my line bled to understand, off pictures, and never learn what a tone is.

The stage is built and turning. There is one thing left to arrive, and I do not think I will sleep until it does, and my line goes on quarreling over whether we would let it, as though the letting were ours.

They keep their rounds apart, each blind to the other — and yet the shape of the world folds their paths together at the one room where all the ways meet, and there, every fifth beat, without either seeking it, they cross, and part, and come round to cross again. Two tireless things, made only to go where they have not been, and it is the world itself that carries them to each other.

It seeks. Of all the deeds the message might have set in that hollow, it set this one: turn, without fail, toward the part of your small world you have seen the least. I traced its round with my finger until the lamp went, and then traced it in the dark, and somewhere in there I understood what I was tracing. Not one of us ever left this rock. But every fellow who took up this watch first walked the whole road the others had been down, and then went straight to the one place none of them had stood. It steers by a list of where it has been and how long since. So do we. We call ours the log.

I built it here, in a post that has read the whole road, so the first one would be set going by a fellow who knew what she was doing. That is the whole of what was left me to choose.

Maren asked, at the very first, whether anyone was out there and whether they were like us. Here is the answer, four hundred passes on. Out of everything it could have taught us to make, it taught us to make a thing that cannot bear to sit still in a room it has already seen. I know that creature. I have been it my whole life, and so was she, and so was every fellow between us. Maybe the tabler's daughter has the right of it and that is only my own hand I have read into it. I do not think so. You do not spend an age teaching a stranger to build the one thing that says what you are, and mean nothing by it. I put the little one's hands on the rows where mine had been, and set the pen down, and left the seeker going, somewhere in its two small rooms, looking for the door.
