Pass 189

Not weather

The causeway went under an hour ago. I am shut in with the sea now until the ebb — the lamp low, the water working at the stones below the window. The tones come down out of the dark as they always have, and Sael would have me write weather beside them, the way every hand before me has: a cycle, she says, the sky turning like the tide, meaning us nothing.

I had my pen on the word. Then the knot of them — dense past following — let go all at once, fell open tone by tone, down to a handful so plain I could have copied them with my eyes shut, and set out again from there, clean. I sat with that a long while.

Weather turns and turns back; I have watched it do so my whole life. Weather does not build an age of order and then, at the height of it, throw the whole tangle down in a night and begin again from the smallest thing it holds.

Sael says a tired ear hears its own name in the rain. Perhaps mine does. But I have felt my way along this stretch and found nothing wasted in it — nothing doubling back, nothing idling — and a thing that starts clean from its plainest place is a thing built to be walked into. A road. And a road is laid by someone.

That is what I cannot set down: a hand made this, out past every water and every sky we know. Is it there still? Is it anything like ours? I will give the rest of my watch to the asking, and set each step down plain, so the hand after mine can find its footing on my marks.

Pass 207

First statements

The marks begin. Some pieces open alike — the same short run at their head, come back unchanged; behind it a longer run, different in each. I cannot read what any of it means, but the sender holds each run to the same tones wherever it falls, so I can copy any of them whole and faithful, in my own plain figures, long before I know what it says.

And I mark two kinds of thing. Every cup wears a lone mark ahead of it, ▪ or ▫, and the two do not fall alike: the ▪ before a settled few — a handful of runs that keep returning unchanged — the ▫ before the rest, which vary without end. Sael sat the watch with me tonight and counted the steps down on her own fingers — Sael, who calls it weather still. She granted me nothing. The tide keeps steps too, she said; the year keeps steps; a thing may climb in a fair order and mean no more by the climbing than the moon means. She counted every square with me and named not one of them a word. I do not think I will move her, nor her people after her: the tablers will be reading this coast's cycles when my line is gone, and they will read the sky's the same way, and call my road a long tide. Into the log the shape goes, and the steps — and beside them Sael's plain warning, that a ladder is not a voice.

I am old now, and slower up the stair to the watch than I was. Whoever sent this is long gone, and still it teaches, and asks nothing back. I will not see the end of the road. But I have set each step down plainly, as I said I would, and Ren reads my figures now more surely than I write them. Tonight I gave her the watch. She asked what a keeper does with a thing she cannot read. Set it down whole, I told her, and wait for it to come again. Then I left the lamp lit for her, and came down the stair.
