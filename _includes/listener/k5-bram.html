Pass 349

The knot

Neru's gone. I was thirty-three years at her elbow. The last four of them I read the rows out to her. I'd read a row and she'd say go on, or she'd say again. That was the whole of how I learned this. So I'm going to keep writing it the way I said it. If it sounds like somebody talking, that's why.

Tonight. It takes two things and ties them into one thing. Then it unties them. There's a mark to pull the first one out and a mark to pull the second one out:

I'm calling it knot. Then it tied a knot inside a knot. Pull the second one out and you don't get a thing, you get another knot. Pull on that one and out comes what's inside:

Right. So I went down to the bench and made one. Two pegs and a cord. Tie a cord round two pegs and you've tied them together. Either peg gets you the cord. The cord gets you the other peg. Then tie the second peg to a third with another cord. Now pull. You can walk it. Peg, cord, peg, cord, peg. As long as you like.

Neru would have asked me what it's for. I don't know yet. I know it's a way to hold any number of things using a thing that only holds two. That's a good trick.

Nine pegs on the bench when I stopped. I've left them there. It was a good trick.

Pass 354 · the boat

Our boat's rotten. Garboard's gone soft the length of her. The steward says there's nothing for a new one this cycle or next. So I'm doing it myself. Beached her in the cut, pulled six planks, and I'm scarfing in what I can get. Most of the pass on that. No reading. I want it down that there was no reading, and why, so nobody after me thinks the wire went quiet.

Doss is dead nine years and I do the tides alone now. The steward has been asked twice for a second hand. She says the post is two on the book. It's been one on the water since Doss.

And I walk the vine alone, which is the part I don't like. Neru taught me that run after she'd stopped being able to feel it. She had the words and I had the hands. I'd put mine on a graft and say quiet. She'd ask quiet how. I'd try to tell her. She'd say that's stirring. Neither of us ever knew whether she was right.

So that's how I have it. Learned off somebody who couldn't check me. There's been nobody to say I'm wrong for nine years, and I hang things out on it.

Anyway. The boat floats. She's ugly. I sailed her round to Tip and back on the ebb and she took nothing. I sat in her a while at the mooring feeling pleased with myself. Then I came up and lit the lamp.

Pass 361

Before and now

New thing tonight. It makes a little holder and puts a count in it. Then you ask the holder and it gives you the count. Then it puts a different count in the same holder. Ask again. Different answer:

That's a vessel. Ask what six joined to one is. It's seven. It was seven before Maren and it'll be seven after me. This one answers differently depending on when you ask. Then it went further and I had to get the lamp trimmed. It set a fork on a vessel. Look at what's in the holder. If it's seven, put eighty-eight in. If it isn't, put ninety-nine in:

Then it ran that same line twice. Same marks, no difference, laid down the one after the other. And the two runs came out different, because the first run changed what the second run was looking at:

…then the very same line again, mark for mark, and the vessel now holding what it just set…

Say that out loud. The same words, said twice. They don't mean the same thing the second time. The first time changed the room. That's not arithmetic any more. Arithmetic doesn't care when you do it. I sat with that. Then I went down and put a chalk mark on the bench. Rubbed it out. Put it back. That's a vessel. I've been doing that with chalk my whole life and never had a word for it.

Now I have the word, and the mark for it: . Make one. Ask it. Set it. Ask it again.

Pass 372

A hole with a name

Rows all season. Lay things in a line, ask for the one at the front:

Fine. Then it asked a row for something that isn't in it. It gave back a mark I haven't seen before. So I chased that mark. It matches itself. It matches nothing else. Not none, not one, not two, not any count I can put against it. It went through a good many to make the point:

…and not any count I could set against it, ever…

On the page it goes as no-answer.

It matches only itself, and no count I can set against it: .

Here's why I like it, and I do like it. When I ask a chest for a thing that isn't in the chest, the chest doesn't tell me anything. It just sits there. Then I'm standing in the store-room deciding whether I looked properly.

This thing answers. It says not here. And it says it so you can't mistake it for a count, because it won't match one. That's a made thing. Somebody had to decide to build a mark for the absence. And make sure it couldn't be mistaken for anything present. They built it because something downstream is going to ask a lot of questions. Some of them won't have answers.

Pass 384

The tireless hand

It has learned to ask what kind of thing a thing is. Hand it a made verb and it says yes. Hand it a count and it says no:

And it works on the marks it was given at the start. Those are verbs too. I hadn't thought about that till it showed me:

Then it used that to build a sameness that looks first and asks after. Is this a doing or a thing? If they're not the same kind, they're not the same. If they're rows, compare them as rows. If they're counts, compare them as counts:

That's the first mark I've met that asks a question about the question before it answers it. It looks at what it's holding before it decides how to look at it.

Row-craft, a long run of it, and none of it hard: how many things a row holds; the thing standing at a named place in it; setting a new thing at the head of a row; the two-way sameness above, put to a hundred rows and true in every one. Copied whole. I wanted the bench.
A two-thing row given a name of its own, and its two parts named first and second. It is the knot again in a plainer coat. Four lines, and I have them.

Then the two I'll be remembered for, if I'm remembered. Take a doing and a row. Lay the doing on every single thing in the row. Get back a row of the answers:

I'm calling it each. Then the other way. Take a doing and a row. Instead of a row back you get one thing. It goes along the row folding each new one into what it's carrying:

One act laid on every item of a row; and a whole row gathered back down to one — , and .

Right. So then I spent the rest of the pass on this. I laid nineteen pegs out on the bench and I did each by hand. Same motion, nineteen times. It took me most of an evening. I got two wrong and had to go back. Then I read the mark again. The mark does that. It doesn't get tired at nineteen. It wouldn't get tired at nine hundred. It never goes back to fix one.

I've spent my life doing the same motion down a line of things. Planking. Cutting. Reading rows out to Neru, one after the next, till she said stop.

This is the first time anybody's written that down as a thing in itself. Not the planks. The going-along.
